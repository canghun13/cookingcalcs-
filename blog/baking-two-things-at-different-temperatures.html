Baking Two Things at Different Temperatures

September 2026 · 6 min read

One oven, two recipes, two different temperatures. Every guide gives the same first half of the answer — split the difference — and then stops, which leaves you with the harder question: if the oven is now at the wrong temperature for both dishes, how long does each one need?

That part has an answer too.

Step 1: The Compromise Temperature

For dishes within about 50°F of each other, set the oven to the midpoint. A dish wanting 400°F and one wanting 450°F share happily at 425°F. Round to the nearest 25°F since that's how oven dials are marked and how most recipes are written.

The reason this works at all is that home ovens are far less precise than recipes imply. A typical oven drifts 15-25°F from its set point and varies by a similar margin between the top and bottom of the cavity — so a 25°F compromise is inside the noise your oven was already producing. Our guide on whether your oven runs hot or cold covers how to check yours.

Step 2: Adjust Each Time

Both dishes are now cooking at a temperature they didn't ask for, so both times move.

New time = original time × (original temp ÷ new temp)

DishRecipe saysAt 425°FChange
Roast vegetables30 min at 450°F30 × (450÷425) = 32 minLonger — it's cooler than it wanted
Chicken thighs40 min at 400°F40 × (400÷425) = 38 minShorter — it's hotter than it wanted

Notice the two move in opposite directions, which is the part that catches people out. Setting one compromise temperature and then using both original times leaves one dish underdone and the other overdone.

Step 3: Stagger the Start

Once you have both adjusted times, work backwards from the longer one. The dish needing 32 minutes goes in first; the 38-minute dish… actually goes in first, since it's longer. The shorter dish joins it when the time remaining matches its own time.

In the example above: chicken thighs at 38 minutes start alone, and the vegetables go in at the 6-minute mark (38 − 32), so both finish together. This is the same subtraction used for staggering vegetables on one sheet pan, and the oven sharing calculator runs it for whatever you're cooking.

Step 4: Add for a Fuller Oven

A loaded oven cooks more slowly than an empty one, and this is the adjustment almost nobody mentions. More food absorbs more heat, dishes block airflow around one another, and the door is open longer while you load and check. Allow roughly 10% extra for two dishes and 15% for three before you start suspecting the recipe was wrong.

When the Gap Is Too Wide

GapWhat to doTrade-off
0-25°FPick either temperatureNone worth worrying about
26-50°FMidpoint, adjust both timesSlight; check both early
51-75°FUse the lower temp, extend the hotter dishLess browning on the hotter dish
Over 75°FCook in sequenceTime, not quality

The reason to favour the lower temperature once the gap widens is asymmetry of failure: a dish that's underdone can be left in longer, while a dish that's burnt is finished. Extending is recoverable; scorching isn't.

The Dishes That Don't Compromise

For most food, temperature is a speed setting — hotter cooks faster. For a handful of things it's a structural instruction, and changing it changes the result rather than the timing.

  • Puff pastry and flaky pastry — need high heat to flash the water in the butter into steam fast enough to separate the layers. Cooler means greasy, not slower.
  • Choux — has to set before it collapses.
  • Soufflé — the rise depends on a specific heat curve.
  • Bread — initial oven spring is temperature-dependent, and many loaves want a hot start specifically.
  • Custards and cheesecake — the opposite problem: too hot and they curdle or crack, and no amount of time adjustment fixes it.

These get the oven alone. Fortunately most of the other dishes in a meal reheat well, so cooking in sequence costs less than it seems — see how long to reheat leftovers for bringing an early dish back up at the end.

Frequently Asked Questions

What temperature do I set the oven to for two dishes?

If the two temperatures are within 50°F of each other, set the oven to roughly the midpoint — 425°F for a dish wanting 400°F and one wanting 450°F. Then adjust each dish's time, because the one that wanted the higher temperature now needs longer and the one that wanted lower comes out sooner.

Should I use the higher or the lower temperature?

The midpoint for gaps under 50°F. For wider gaps, use the lower temperature and give the hotter dish more time, since undercooking can be fixed by leaving something in longer while burning cannot be undone. The exception is a dish whose whole point is a crisp or browned surface, which needs the higher heat or it should be cooked separately.

How much longer does a dish take at a lower temperature?

Multiply the original time by the original temperature divided by the new one. A dish needing 40 minutes at 425°F, cooked at 400°F, becomes 40 × (425 ÷ 400) ≈ 42 minutes. Published rules of thumb range from this ratio to a flatter 10-15% per 25°F, so treat any figure as the point to start checking.

Do I need to change the temperature at all if the dishes are close?

Within about 25°F, no. Most home ovens vary by that much across the cavity anyway, and many drift 15-25°F from their set point. Pick either temperature, or the midpoint, and just watch both dishes.

Why did both dishes take longer than the calculator said?

A fuller oven cooks more slowly. More food absorbs more heat, dishes block airflow around each other, and the door stays open longer while you load and check. Roughly 10% extra for two dishes and 15% for three is a reasonable allowance before you start blaming the recipe.
