Direct vs Indirect Heat: When to Use Each

Updated August 2026 · 6 min read

Every grilling recipe eventually tells you to use "direct" or "indirect" heat, and mixing the two up is one of the most common ways a cook ends up with a burnt outside and a raw center. The difference is simple once it clicks: direct heat sears, indirect heat cooks through.

The Core Difference

Direct HeatIndirect Heat
Food positionRight over the flame/coalsBeside the flame, not over it
Typical temp400-550°F+250-350°F
What it's good forSearing, browning, quick cooksCooking through without burning the surface
Best forBurgers, steaks, chops, fish, shrimp, thin vegetablesWhole chickens, ribs, roasts, brisket

Why Size Decides Which One You Need

The deciding factor is almost always how long the food needs to cook. Anything that finishes in under 20-25 minutes can usually sit directly over the heat — the outside and inside reach done at roughly the same time. Anything larger or slower-cooking will burn on the outside long before the center catches up if it sits directly over high heat, which is exactly why whole chickens, ribs, and roasts need indirect heat: it cooks the food more like a closed oven, giving the inside time to reach temperature without torching the surface.

Setting Up Both Zones

On a charcoal grill, bank all the lit coals onto one half of the grate and leave the other half empty — the coal side is direct, the empty side is indirect. On a gas grill, light only some of the burners (for a 3-burner grill, light one or two) and place food needing indirect heat over the unlit burner, lid closed. Either setup lets you move food between zones mid-cook, which is exactly what thick cuts need.

Using Both in One Cook

Thick steaks, bone-in chicken pieces, and pork chops often benefit from both zones in a single cook: sear hard over direct heat for 2-3 minutes per side to build color and crust, then move to indirect heat with the lid closed to finish cooking through gently. This avoids the classic problem of a beautifully charred outside wrapped around a cold or undercooked center. Flare-Ups Are a Direct-Heat Problem

A flare-up happens when dripping fat hits an open flame or hot coals directly beneath the food — which is only possible in a direct-heat zone, since indirect heat by definition has no food sitting over the fire. Fattier cuts (chicken thighs, sausage, marbled steaks) flare up more than lean ones. If flames climb up over the food, the fix is to move it to the indirect side temporarily rather than closing the lid over an active flare — closing the lid on a real flare-up can spike the temperature well past what you're aiming for and singe the surface. Once the flare settles, move the food back to finish over direct heat.

Lid Open or Closed Changes Which Zone You're Really In

With the lid open, direct heat is radiant heat straight from the flame or coals — indirect heat barely exists, since there's no enclosed space to circulate hot air to the far side of the grill. Closing the lid turns the whole grill into something closer to a convection oven: the indirect side heats up more evenly and effectively, while the direct side gets an added boost from reflected heat off the lid. This is why indirect cooking (whole chicken, ribs, roasts) is almost always done lid-closed, and why quick direct-heat searing is often done lid-open, especially for thin cuts where a fast, high-heat crust matters more than even heating.

What Goes Wrong With Each Mistake

Using direct heat on something that needed indirect: the surface chars or blackens well before the center is safe to eat — this is the single most common cause of a "burnt outside, raw inside" result with whole chickens, ribs, and thick roasts.

Using indirect heat on something that needed direct: the food cooks through fine, but never develops the sear, char, or crust that direct heat provides — a burger or steak cooked entirely indirect will taste more steamed than grilled.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I cook a whole chicken over direct heat?

The skin will burn and blacken long before the inside reaches a safe 165°F — a whole chicken is thick enough that direct heat cooks the outside far faster than the center. Move it to indirect heat (beside the flame, lid closed) so it cooks more like an oven.

Can I use both direct and indirect heat in the same cook?

Yes — this is the standard approach for thick steaks, chicken pieces, and pork chops. Sear over direct heat for color and crust, then move to the indirect side to finish cooking through gently without burning the surface.

How do I set up direct and indirect zones on a charcoal grill?

Bank all the lit coals to one half of the grill, leaving the other half empty. The side with coals underneath is your direct zone; the empty side is indirect. On a gas grill, light only some of the burners and leave the food over the unlit ones for indirect heat.
