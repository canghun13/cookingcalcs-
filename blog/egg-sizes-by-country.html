Egg Sizes by Country: What "Large" Means in the US, UK, Canada, Australia and Japan

November 2026 · 6 min read

"Large" means a different egg in every country. A US or Canadian large egg weighs about 57 g in the shell, a UK or European large 63–73 g, and an Australian large 50–58 g. When a recipe comes from another country, match the weight, not the name: a US large is a UK medium, an Australian extra-large, or a Japanese M.

Egg sizes are set by each country's grading rules, and they were never coordinated. The US grades by minimum weight per dozen, Canada by minimum weight per egg, the UK and EU by weight bands, Australia by the weight of a 12-egg carton, and Japan by letter grades. The names overlap, the weights don't.

Weights are per egg in the shell. US figures are the USDA weight classes; Canadian, European, Australian, New Zealand and Japanese bands follow each country's grading standard as summarized in the Wikipedia overview of chicken egg sizes. Australian cartons are sold by the dozen's weight, which is why the boxes say 600 g or 700 g rather than a size per egg. The UK's numbered sizes 1–7 from before 1996 are covered in old UK egg sizes.

The Swaps People Ask About Most

  • US recipe, cooking in the UK: use medium eggs. Three US large eggs (171 g) are three UK medium (about 174 g); three UK large eggs would be about 204 g.
  • UK recipe, cooking in the US: use extra-large or jumbo for a UK large. For a UK medium, US large is the match.
  • Australian recipe, cooking in the US: US large works one for one. Australian recipe writers mostly assume eggs of 50–60 g, and RecipeTin Eats notes that Australian large eggs are about 50–55 g each.
  • US recipe, cooking in Australia: extra-large (700 g carton) eggs are the closest to a US large; a 600 g carton's large eggs are a little small for baking with four or more.
  • Japanese recipe, cooking elsewhere: Japanese recipes usually assume M or L eggs (58–70 g), so a US extra-large, a UK large or an Australian extra-large.

When It Doesn't Matter

With one or two eggs in a recipe, the difference between countries is 5–15 g — less than the natural variation inside one carton. It matters in recipes built on eggs: sponges, custards, soufflés, meringues, quiches, enriched breads, and anything with four or more eggs. For those, weigh the cracked eggs: a recipe written for US large eggs wants about 50 g of whole egg per egg. The egg weight converter turns a weight of beaten egg back into a count, and egg whites and yolks by size covers recipes that use only one part. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a large egg the same size in every country?

No. A US or Canadian large egg is about 57 g in the shell, a UK or European large egg is 63–73 g, and an Australian large egg is only 50–58 g. Japan doesn't use the word at all; its M and L grades cover 58–70 g.

What size egg should I use for a US recipe in the UK?

A UK medium (53–63 g) is the closest match to a US large (57 g). Using UK large eggs adds about 10 g per egg, which matters in recipes with four or more eggs.

What size egg should I use for an Australian recipe in the US?

Australian recipes are usually written for eggs of about 50–60 g, so a US large (57 g) works one for one. Australian 'extra large' (700 g cartons, about 60 g each) is close to a US large too.

What are Japanese M and L eggs in US sizes?

Japanese M eggs (58–64 g) sit between a US large and extra-large; L eggs (64–70 g) match a US extra-large; LL (70–76 g) is a US jumbo.
