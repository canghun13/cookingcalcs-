How Long to Cook Pork Shoulder — Pulled Pork Times by Weight and Temperature

July 2026 · 6 min read

Pork shoulder cook time estimates online swing from 30 minutes to 2 hours per pound — a four-fold difference for what sounds like the same cut. Almost all of that gap comes down to oven temperature, and what doneness the recipe is actually targeting.

These times target fully pulled pork (195-205°F internal). Boneless shoulder cooks somewhat faster than bone-in at the same weight; start checking toward the lower end of the range if boneless. A common approach is starting hot (450°F for 20 minutes) to build a crust, then dropping to the low temperature for the remainder — the times above assume the low-temperature portion of that process.

The Real Reason Estimates Vary So Much

Beyond oven temperature, the biggest hidden variable is which doneness a recipe is actually targeting. "Pork shoulder" recipes online are really describing two different dishes with two different timelines:

TargetInternal TempResult
Slicing180°F (82°C)Firm enough to slice into portions, less shreddable
Pulling195-205°F (91-96°C)Falls apart easily, classic pulled pork texture

Both are safe — pork shoulder is safe to eat far below either number — the gap is purely about how much collagen has broken down. A recipe timed for 180°F slicing will read as significantly "faster" than one timed for 205°F pulling, even on the same size roast at the same oven temperature, simply because it's stopping earlier. This is the same collagen logic behind brisket and ribs needing far more than their safe minimum — pork shoulder is just the largest example of it on this site.

The Stall

Like brisket, pork shoulder commonly plateaus somewhere in the 160-170°F range for an hour or more as surface moisture evaporates and cools the meat, counteracting the oven's heat. This is normal and not a sign of a problem — pushing the oven temperature up to force through it tends to dry out the exterior rather than actually speeding things up. Patience, or wrapping in foil partway through to trap steam, are the standard ways through it.

Judging Doneness Without a Thermometer

For bone-in shoulder, the classic test is the bone: grip it and twist. If it pulls free with almost no resistance, the roast is done. Beyond that, press a fork into the thickest part — it should slide in easily and twist the meat into shreds with minimal effort. If the meat still feels firm and springs back, or the bone fights you, it needs more time regardless of what the clock or the chart above says.

Resting and Holding

Rest for at least 30-45 minutes before pulling — this large a roast holds heat well and benefits from more rest than a smaller cut. If it finishes early, wrap it in foil and towels and hold it in a cooler; it will stay safely hot for 2-5 hours, which makes pork shoulder unusually forgiving for timing around a meal versus most large roasts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does pork shoulder take to cook per pound?

About 90 minutes per pound at 250°F, or roughly 2 hours per pound at 225°F, for fully pulled pork. At 300°F, expect closer to 60 minutes per pound. These are estimates, not guarantees — always finish by internal temperature, not the clock, since pork shoulder can stall for an hour or more partway through.

What's the difference between 180°F and 200°F for pork shoulder?

180°F gives meat firm enough to slice, while 195-205°F breaks down enough connective tissue for the meat to pull apart easily with a fork. Both are safe — pork shoulder is safe well below either number — the difference is purely texture. Most pulled pork recipes target the higher range specifically because pulling requires that extra collagen breakdown.

Why do pork shoulder cook time estimates vary so much online?

Mainly because recipes don't always specify oven temperature, whether the roast is bone-in or boneless, and which doneness they're targeting. A 250°F cook to 180°F (slicing) is a very different timeline than a 225°F cook to 205°F (pulling) — both are correct for what they're describing, but comparing their times directly is misleading.

How do I know when pork shoulder is done without a thermometer?

The bone should twist and pull free with almost no resistance if the shoulder is bone-in. The meat should also feel soft enough that a fork slides in easily and twists to shred it. If the bone still fights you or the meat feels firm and springy, it needs more time regardless of what the clock says.
