How Long to Cook Ribs — Times by Rib Type and Oven Temperature

July 2026 · 6 min read

Rib cook times vary more by cut than by weight — a rack of baby backs and a rack of spare ribs can weigh similarly but finish an hour apart. Like brisket, ribs are a connective-tissue cut, so they're done when the collagen breaks down, not when they hit a food-safety number.

Times assume the ribs are wrapped in foil for most of the cook. Add 20-30 minutes uncovered at the end if you're saucing and want caramelized edges. Going above 300°F doesn't reliably save time — it dries the exterior before the interior collagen has had a chance to break down, which is the same trap as rushing a brisket.

Why Ribs Need 190-203°F, Not 145°F

Pork is safe at 145°F, and ribs are no exception — but ribs pulled at 145°F are tough and chewy. That's because ribs are loaded with collagen, the connective protein that only converts to soft gelatin after sustained time above roughly 190°F. The extra 45-60 degrees isn't about safety at all; it's the entire difference between ribs that fight you and ribs that pull cleanly off the bone.

Internal TempResult
145°F (63°C)Safe to eat, but tough — collagen intact
190-195°F (88-90°C)Tender with some bite, meat still clings to bone
200-203°F (93-95°C)Fall-off-the-bone soft

Which end of that range you want is genuinely a preference. Competition barbecue often targets the lower end, where the meat holds together when bitten; most home cooks prefer the upper end. See the Meat Temperature Guide for how this compares to other cuts.

The Bend Test (No Thermometer Needed)

Ribs are one of the few cuts where a physical test is as reliable as a thermometer, since a probe can easily hit bone and read wrong. Lift the rack from one end with tongs: done ribs bow deeply and the surface visibly cracks, while underdone ribs stay stiff and flat. Two more signs — the meat has pulled back about ¼ to ½ inch from the bone ends, and a toothpick slides between the bones with almost no resistance.

Remove the Membrane First

The thin silverskin membrane on the bone side doesn't render or soften during cooking, so it stays chewy and blocks rub and smoke from reaching the meat underneath. Slide a butter knife under it at one end, grip with a paper towel, and pull it off in one sheet. This single step does more for rib texture than most rub or sauce choices.

The 3-2-1 Method, and When It's Too Much

The popular 3-2-1 method — 3 hours unwrapped, 2 hours wrapped, 1 hour unwrapped with sauce — was developed for full spare ribs on a smoker at 225°F. Applied to baby backs, which are smaller and leaner, it usually overcooks them into mush. For baby backs, 2-2-1 or simply the times in the chart above works better.

Grill and Smoker

Ribs on a grill follow the same logic: indirect heat at 250-300°F, same 190-203°F target, same several-hour timeline. See Direct vs Indirect Heat for setting up an indirect zone, and the Grill Temperature & Time Calculator for the hand test if your grill has no thermometer.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to cook baby back ribs at 275°F?

About 2½ to 3 hours wrapped in foil at 275°F, plus 20-30 minutes uncovered at the end if you want caramelized edges. Spare ribs and St. Louis cut take longer — roughly 3 to 3½ hours at the same temperature — because they're larger and have more connective tissue.

What internal temperature should ribs be cooked to?

190-203°F (88-95°C) for tender, pull-apart ribs — far above pork's 145°F safe minimum. Ribs are safe to eat at 145°F, but the collagen in them doesn't break down into gelatin until they spend time in the 190-203°F range, which is what makes the difference between chewy ribs and tender ones.

Should I cover ribs with foil in the oven?

Yes for most of the cook. Wrapping traps steam, which speeds up collagen breakdown and keeps the meat from drying out over several hours. Unwrap for the last 20-30 minutes — optionally with sauce and the broiler on — to firm up the surface and get caramelized edges, since foil-wrapped ribs alone finish soft and pale.

How do I know when ribs are done without a thermometer?

Use the bend test: lift the rack from one end with tongs. Done ribs bow deeply and the surface cracks; underdone ribs stay stiff. The meat should also have pulled back about ¼ to ½ inch from the ends of the bones. A toothpick should slide between the bones with almost no resistance.
