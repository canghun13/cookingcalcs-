How Long to Cook Tempeh and Seitan

July 2026 · 6 min read

Tempeh and seitan are both already technically edible straight from the package — tempeh is steamed during fermentation, and packaged seitan is fully cooked. So "how long to cook" them isn't really a food safety question, it's a texture question: how long until they're properly browned, crisped, or heated through for the dish you're making.

Tempeh Cook Times by Method

MethodTempTimeResult
Pan-sear (sliced/strips)Medium-high2–3 min per sideCrispiest, fastest
Oven-baked (cubed/sliced)400°F (200°C)20–25 min, flip halfwayHands-off, even cooking
Air fryer (cubed)375°F (190°C)10–12 min, shake halfwayCrisp without much oil
Steamed (whole block, before other methods)Simmer10 minSoftens texture, reduces bitterness

Tempeh's firm, dense texture holds up well to high heat, which is why pan-searing gives the crispiest result in the least time. If a batch tastes noticeably bitter, steam it for 10 minutes before searing, baking, or air frying — this step softens the flavor and helps marinades penetrate better.

Seitan Cook Times by Method

MethodTempTimeResult
Pan-sear (sliced/strips)Medium-high2–4 min per sideBrowned crust, chewy interior
Oven-baked (whole piece/cubed)400°F (200°C)15–20 min, flip halfwayFirmer, drier crust
Air fryer (cubed)375°F (190°C)8–10 min, shake halfwayCrispiest exterior
Simmered (homemade, from raw dough)Gentle simmer45–60 minCooks the raw gluten dough through

Store-bought seitan is already fully cooked, so any of the quick methods (pan-sear, bake, air fry) are purely for browning and crust. Homemade seitan made from raw vital wheat gluten dough is the exception — that needs a full 45-60 minute simmer to actually cook the gluten structure through, since it starts as a raw dough rather than a pre-cooked product.

Tempeh vs Seitan: Cooking Differences

Tempeh is a whole-bean product bound by mycelium (the fermentation culture), so it's naturally porous and absorbs marinades quickly — but it can also fall apart if handled too roughly while hot. Seitan is a solid mass of developed gluten, so it holds its shape much better under high heat and vigorous stirring, but it absorbs marinade more slowly since its surface is denser. If you're marinating either, give tempeh 30 minutes and seitan several hours (or overnight) for the flavor to actually penetrate.

Common Mistakes

Skipping the steam step on bitter tempeh. If a brand or batch tastes sharp or bitter, a quick 10-minute steam before the main cooking method fixes most of it — this step is easy to skip since it's not required for safety, but it makes a real difference in flavor.

Treating both like meat and checking for a safe temperature. Neither tempeh nor seitan has a food-safety minimum temperature to hit, since both are already safe to eat as-is. Cook by color and texture instead of chasing a number on a thermometer.

Overcrowding the pan or air fryer basket. Both need direct contact with hot surfaces or circulating air to crisp properly — piled up or overlapping pieces steam instead of browning, leaving a soft, pale result.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do tempeh and seitan need to be cooked before eating?

Both are technically pre-cooked during production — tempeh is steamed as part of fermentation, and store-bought seitan is fully cooked when packaged. Neither needs cooking for food safety. Cooking them is entirely about flavor and texture: browning, crisping, and absorbing marinade.

Why does tempeh taste bitter, and does cooking method fix it?

Bitterness in tempeh usually comes from the fermentation process itself, and it's more noticeable in some brands or batches than others. Steaming tempeh for 10 minutes before pan-searing, baking, or air frying it noticeably softens the bitter edge and also helps the tempeh absorb marinade better, since steaming opens up the surface.

How do I know when tempeh or seitan is done cooking?

By color and texture, not temperature — since both are already safe to eat raw, there's no safe minimum internal temperature to hit. Tempeh is done when it's deep golden-brown and firm on the outside. Seitan is done when it develops a browned crust; the interior stays chewy by design regardless of cook time.

Can I air fry tempeh and seitan the same way?

Similar approach, different times. Both do well sliced or cubed, tossed lightly in oil, in a single layer at 375-400°F. Tempeh typically needs 10-12 minutes; seitan, being denser and already fully cooked, often needs slightly less time to develop a good crust — closer to 8-10 minutes.
