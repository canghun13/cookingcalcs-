How to Scale a Recipe Up or Down

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Scaling factor = servings you want ÷ servings the recipe makes. Multiply every ingredient by it. A recipe for 4 made for 10 is ×2.5; a recipe for 6 made for 2 is ×0.33. The arithmetic is the easy part — this page covers the parts that don't follow it: eggs, leavening, salt, pans and cooking time.

The recipe multiplier applies the factor to a whole ingredient list and rounds to measurable amounts. Halving has its own page with every ingredient covered: how to cut a recipe in half.

Worked Example: Scaling Up

A recipe for 4 uses 2 cups flour, 1 cup sugar, ½ cup butter, 2 eggs and 1 tsp baking powder. For 10 people the factor is 2.5:

  • Flour: 2 × 2.5 = 5 cups
  • Sugar: 1 × 2.5 = 2½ cups
  • Butter: ½ × 2.5 = 1¼ cups (2½ sticks)
  • Eggs: 2 × 2.5 = 5 eggs
  • Baking powder: 1 × 2.5 = 2½ tsp

Scaling Down: Turning Fractions Into Spoons

Small factors produce amounts no measuring cup has. Convert to tablespoons first (1 cup = 16 tbsp, 1 tbsp = 3 tsp) and the fractions become countable — a third of ¾ cup is a third of 12 tablespoons, which is 4.

Better still, scale by weight. 250 g × 0.33 is 83 g, exactly; ⅓ cup × 0.33 is an awkward guess. If a recipe only gives cups, convert with the cups to grams converter before scaling.

Eggs

Eggs are the one ingredient you can't simply multiply. A large egg is about 50 g out of the shell, roughly 3 tablespoons beaten, so:

  • Half an egg: beat one and use about 1½ tablespoons (25 g).
  • ⅓ of 2 eggs (0.67 egg): one small egg, or a large egg minus about a tablespoon.
  • 2.5 eggs: two eggs plus half of a beaten third.

For drier bakes like cookies and scones, round down; for cakes and quick breads, rounding up by part of an egg rarely hurts. Different egg sizes are covered in how to substitute egg sizes.

Leavening, Salt and Spices

Baking powder and baking soda: scale them exactly when halving, doubling or tripling — they are not forgiving, and a small excess of soda tastes soapy and metallic. Use a ⅛-teaspoon measure for small batches. Only for very large batches (several times the recipe, usually in a bakery) do some bakers trim leavening slightly, because a deep pan of batter behaves differently.

Salt and strong spices: start with about three-quarters of the calculated amount, then taste and add. Cayenne, cloves, cinnamon and star anise are easy to overdo, and you can always add more but never take it out.

In a correctly sized pan, keep the original oven temperature and start checking for doneness about 10 minutes early — smaller pans finish a little sooner because the heat has less distance to travel. If you have to use the original pan for a half recipe, the layer will be thinner and bake noticeably faster. The pan size converter works out other combinations.

Cooking Time

Baked goods: roughly the same time in a pan of the right size, because depth stays the same. Two pans of the same item bake in about the same time as one, though a crowded oven may need a few extra minutes and a rotation halfway.

Soups, sauces and stews: a smaller batch comes to temperature and reduces faster — expect 15–25% less time for a half batch and keep the heat moderate so it doesn't scorch. A bigger batch takes longer to come to a simmer, but once simmering it cooks at the same rate.

Roasts and braises: time follows thickness, not weight. A smaller roast cooks faster, but not in proportion — go by internal temperature, using the meat temperature guide.

How Far a Recipe Will Scale

Most recipes scale reliably from about half to double. Beyond that the math still works, but pans, pots and oven space stop cooperating, and cooking times drift outside what the recipe was tested for. For a big crowd it's usually better to make the recipe several times at its original size. Going down, single servings work well on the stovetop but are hard in baking, where tiny amounts are hard to measure.

When Not to Scale Down — Freeze Instead

Yeast doughs, soufflés, meringues and sauces that need volume to reduce properly don't shrink well. Make the full batch and freeze the extra: most soups, stews, casseroles, muffins and quick breads keep 2–3 months in the freezer.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate a scaling factor?

Divide the servings you want by the servings the recipe makes. 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 to scale up; 2 ÷ 6 = 0.33 to scale down. Multiply every ingredient by the factor.

How do I reduce a recipe by a third?

Multiply every ingredient by 0.33, converting cups to tablespoons first so the fractions are measurable. Scale leavening exactly, start the salt and spices a little low, and handle eggs by volume.

Do I double the baking powder when I double a recipe?

Yes. For home batches — halving, doubling, even tripling — scale baking powder and soda exactly. Only very large commercial batches sometimes trim it.

Does cooking time double when I double a recipe?

No. Baked goods in a pan of the right size take about the same time. Larger pots of soup take longer to come to a simmer but not twice as long. Roasts follow thickness, so check internal temperature.

Should I lower the oven temperature for a smaller pan?

No — keep the same temperature and start checking about 10 minutes early. Lowering the temperature is a fix for glass pans or very deep batter, not for a smaller pan.

Can I reduce a recipe to 1 serving?

Usually on the stovetop. Baking is harder because small amounts of batter and leavening are hard to measure; making the full recipe and freezing portions is often easier.
