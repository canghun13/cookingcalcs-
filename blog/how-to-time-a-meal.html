How to Time a Meal So Everything Finishes Together

September 2026 · 7 min read

Cooking one dish well is a solved problem — the recipe tells you what to do. Cooking four dishes so they arrive hot at the same moment is a different skill, and no recipe covers it because each one is written as though it has your kitchen to itself.

Every guide to this says "work backwards" and then stops, which leaves the actual work undone. Here is the whole method.

The Rule

Start prep at = serving time − rest − cook − prep

Subtract in that order, for each dish independently. Serving time is the only fixed point in the meal; everything else is scheduled relative to it.

DishPrepCookRestPrep startsIn the oven
Roast chicken15 min80 min20 min4:354:50
Roast potatoes10 min45 min—5:355:45
Green beans5 min12 min—6:136:18 (stovetop)

Serving at 6:30pm. Sorted by start time, the afternoon has a shape: chicken at 4:35, nothing to do for an hour, potatoes at 5:35, beans at 6:13.

The point of writing it out isn't the individual numbers — it's that the dishes interact, and those interactions are invisible until the times sit next to each other. The meal timeline planner does the sorting and flags the conflicts.

Plan Backwards, Not Forwards

Planning forwards — "I'll start at four and see where we get to" — tells you when dinner will happen. Planning backwards makes dinner happen when you want it to. That difference matters most for the thing forwards planning hides: whether the plan is possible at all.

Work backwards and an oven conflict shows up while you're still holding a pen. Work forwards and it shows up at 5:45 with a tray of potatoes in your hands and a chicken occupying the oven for another 25 minutes.

The Resting Window Is the Whole Trick

Look at the table again. The chicken leaves the oven at 6:10 and rests until 6:30. That's 20 minutes of empty oven arriving at precisely the moment the sides need heat — and at whatever temperature you like, since nothing is cooking in it.

This is the single most useful structural feature of a meal with a roast in it, and treating the rest as dead time is why sides so often arrive lukewarm. Cooks who skip resting to "save time" carve a roast that leaks its juices onto the board and still serve cool sides, losing twice.

So schedule into the rest deliberately. Roast potatoes finishing, a gratin getting its browning pass, bread warming, anything cooked earlier coming back up. Resting times for each cut are in our meat temperature guide.

Where Plans Actually Break

FailureCauseFix
Two dishes want the oven at onceOverlapping windows, incompatible temperaturesUnder 50°F apart, share at the midpoint. Over that, move one into the resting window
Everything ran 20 minutes lateFuller oven cooks slower; prep took longerAdd 10-15% oven time and a 15-30 min buffer
Sides cold at the tableFinished too early with nowhere to goSchedule crisp things last; use the rest window
Roast done 40 minutes earlyCooked to the clock, not temperatureHold it — a rested roast keeps 45+ min tented
Nothing prepped when cooking startsPrep time left out of the planPrep is a scheduled block, not a free action

The last one is the most common. People schedule cooking times and treat chopping as something that happens instantly between dishes. Prep for four dishes at once takes considerably longer than prep for one, because you're also washing the board and finding the second baking tray.

The Order to Cook In

When the oven is the bottleneck — and it usually is — sequence by what tolerates waiting rather than by what feels most important.

WhenWhatWhy
FirstAnything needing a long rest; anything that can't share the ovenThe rest frees the oven; pastry and bread need it alone anyway
MiddleCasseroles, gratins, dense sidesHold heat well, tolerate sitting covered
LastRoast vegetables, anything crisp, green vegetablesLose texture within about ten minutes of coming out

Anything that finishes early and holds badly can be finished in the resting window instead. And most sides reheat well enough that cooking something an hour ahead and bringing it back up for ten minutes costs almost nothing — see how long to reheat leftovers.

A Note on Buffers

Add 15 to 30 minutes of slack to any multi-dish plan. Two things push meals late and neither is the recipe's fault: a loaded oven cooks more slowly because the food absorbs heat and blocks airflow, and prep runs long. Being 20 minutes early costs you nothing. Being 20 minutes late with no buffer makes everything downstream late as well, and that's when people start turning ovens up and burning things.

Frequently Asked Questions

What time should I start cooking for a dinner at 6:30?

Take your longest dish and subtract its resting time, its cooking time, and its prep time from 6:30. A chicken needing 15 minutes prep, 80 minutes roasting and 20 minutes rest means prep starts at 4:35 and it goes into the oven at 4:50. Every other dish is scheduled the same way, then sorted.

Should I plan forwards or backwards?

Backwards, always. Serving time is the fixed point and everything else moves around it. Planning forwards from when you start cooking tells you when dinner will happen rather than making it happen when you want, and it hides oven conflicts until you are already in them.

What do I do while the roast is resting?

Use the oven it just freed. A 30-minute rest is 30 minutes of empty oven at exactly the moment your sides need heat, at whatever temperature you choose. Roast potatoes, gratins, bread and anything being reheated all belong in that window.

How much buffer should I add to a meal plan?

15 to 30 minutes for a multi-dish meal. A fuller oven cooks more slowly, and prep for several dishes at once takes longer than prep for one. Being early costs you nothing; being late with no slack makes everything after the delay late too.

Which dish should come out of the oven last?

Whatever loses its texture fastest — roast vegetables, roast potatoes, anything crisp. These go from excellent to disappointing within about ten minutes of coming out. Dense dishes like casseroles hold their heat and tolerate sitting covered, so they can come out earlier.
