Labor Cost Percentage Word Problems: Restaurant Labor Math Step by Step

November 2026 · 10 min read

Labor cost % = labor cost ÷ sales × 100. Labor cost includes hourly wages, salaries, overtime, payroll taxes and benefits for the same period as the sales. Most restaurants run between 25% and 35%; add the food cost and you have prime cost, which most aim to keep near 60% of sales.

Labor cost problems appear in hospitality cost-control courses right after food cost percentage, and they use the same idea: a cost divided by the sales it produced. The problems below go from a single percentage to building a schedule that hits a target. Benchmarks are industry averages — Toast puts a typical restaurant at about 30% of sales, with fine dining higher and quick service lower — so a textbook problem's target is whatever the problem says.

The Formulas

Labor cost % = labor cost ÷ sales × 100
Labor cost = (hours × hourly wage) + salaries + taxes and benefits
Labor budget = projected sales × target %
Prime cost = cost of goods sold + labor cost
Sales per labor hour = sales ÷ labor hours

Level 1: The Percentage

A restaurant had $42,000 in sales last week and $12,180 in labor cost. What was the labor cost percentage?

$12,180 ÷ $42,000 = 0.29 = 29%.

Level 2: Building Labor Cost From Hours

Five cooks each work 32 hours at $18. Two dishwashers each work 25 hours at $16. The kitchen manager is paid a salary of $1,150 a week. Payroll taxes and benefits add 10% to all pay. Sales were $18,500. What is the labor cost percentage?

Cooks: 5 × 32 × $18 = $2,880. Dishwashers: 2 × 25 × $16 = $800. Manager: $1,150. Wages = $4,830.

With taxes and benefits: $4,830 × 1.10 = $5,313. Labor cost % = $5,313 ÷ $18,500 = 28.7%.

Leaving out the salary and the 10% would give $3,680 ÷ $18,500 = 19.9% — a number that looks great and is wrong. Labor cost means everything the staff costs, not only the hourly wages.

Level 3: A Labor Budget From a Target

Next week's sales are projected at $24,000 and the labor target is 30%. Salaried staff cost $1,800. The average hourly wage, including taxes, is $17.50. How many hourly labor hours can be scheduled?

Labor budget = $24,000 × 0.30 = $7,200. Left for hourly staff = $7,200 − $1,800 = $5,400.

Hours = $5,400 ÷ $17.50 = 308.57, so schedule 308 hours. Round hours down — rounding up puts you over budget.

Level 4: Overtime

A line cook earns $20 an hour and works 46 hours in one week. Overtime is paid at 1.5 times the hourly rate for hours over 40. What is the cook's pay, and how much did overtime add?

Regular: 40 × $20 = $800. Overtime: 6 × ($20 × 1.5) = 6 × $30 = $180. Pay = $980.

At the straight rate the same 46 hours would cost $920, so overtime added $60. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, overtime is due after 40 hours in a workweek; some states also count daily overtime.

Level 5: Prime Cost

A month's sales were $30,000. Cost of goods sold was $9,300 and labor cost was $8,700. What is the prime cost and the prime cost percentage?

Prime cost = $9,300 + $8,700 = $18,000. Prime cost % = $18,000 ÷ $30,000 = 60%.

Split out: food cost 31%, labor 29%. Prime cost is the number most owners watch, because food and labor are the two costs a manager can change week to week.

Level 6: Sales Per Labor Hour

A restaurant schedules 450 labor hours a week at an average of $16 an hour. In a normal week sales are $22,500. In a slow week sales drop to $18,000 but the schedule does not change. Compare sales per labor hour and labor cost %. How many hours would bring the slow week back to 32%?

Labor cost = 450 × $16 = $7,200.

Normal week: $22,500 ÷ 450 = $50 per labor hour; $7,200 ÷ $22,500 = 32%.

Slow week: $18,000 ÷ 450 = $40 per labor hour; $7,200 ÷ $18,000 = 40%.

Budget at 32% = $18,000 × 0.32 = $5,760. Hours = $5,760 ÷ $16 = 360 hours, so 90 fewer hours. That is why schedules are built from sales forecasts, not copied from last week.

Common Mistakes

  • Counting only hourly wages. Salaries, payroll taxes and benefits are labor cost too.
  • Mismatched periods. A week of labor divided by a month of sales gives a meaningless percentage.
  • Rounding scheduled hours up. Round the hours you can afford down.
  • Forgetting overtime when building a schedule. One cook at 46 hours costs more than two cooks splitting the same hours.

Labor Cost Percentage Word Problems — Answer Key

  1. 30%. $2,550 ÷ $8,500 = 0.30 = 30%.
  2. $15,000. $60,000 × 0.25 = $15,000.
  3. $722. 38 × $19 = $722.
  4. $782. 40 × $17 = $680. 4 × $25.50 = $102. $680 + $102 = $782.
  5. 31.7%. $11,400 ÷ $36,000 = 0.3167 = 31.7%.
  6. $20,700; 62.7%. $10,800 + $9,900 = $20,700. $20,700 ÷ $33,000 = 62.7%.
  7. 250 hours. $6,000 − $1,500 = $4,500. $4,500 ÷ $18 = 250 hours.
  8. $50 per labor hour. $28,000 ÷ 560 = $50.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good labor cost percentage for a restaurant?

Most restaurants run between 25% and 35% of sales, with about 30% a common benchmark. Quick service tends to be lower (about 20–25%) and fine dining higher (about 35–45%).

Does labor cost include payroll taxes and benefits?

Yes. Labor cost covers hourly wages, salaries, overtime, employer payroll taxes and benefits for the period. Using hourly wages alone makes the percentage look lower than it is.

What is prime cost?

Prime cost is cost of goods sold plus total labor cost. With $9,300 of food cost and $8,700 of labor on $30,000 of sales, prime cost is $18,000, or 60% of sales.

How do you work out how many hours you can schedule?

Multiply projected sales by the labor target to get the budget, subtract salaried pay, then divide by the average hourly wage including taxes. Round the result down.
