Oven Rack Positions Explained

September 2026 · 6 min read

Search for which oven rack is hottest and you'll get both answers with equal confidence. Some sources say heat rises so the top is hottest. Others say the element is at the bottom so the bottom is hottest. It's an unusually persistent disagreement for something so basic.

They're both right, and the reason they're both right is the useful part.

An Oven Gives You Two Kinds of Heat

Radiant heat comes directly off the heating element as infrared energy, and it drops off sharply with distance. In most home ovens the element sits at the bottom, so a pan on the lower rack is receiving considerably more of it than one on the top.

Convected heat is the hot air filling the cavity. That air rises, so the ambient temperature near the top of the oven runs warmer than near the bottom — and the top rack is also closest to the broiler element.

So "which rack is hotter" has no single answer, because the two racks are hotter in different ways. The bottom delivers more heat into the base of the pan. The top surrounds food with hotter air and browns its surface faster. Once you frame it that way, rack choice stops being folklore and becomes a straightforward question: which surface of this food needs the most heat?

What Goes Where

RackBest forBecause
BottomPizza, pie crust, bread, anything with a base that must crispDirect radiant heat hits the pan bottom hardest
MiddleCakes, cookies, casseroles, roasts — and anything a recipe doesn't specifyThe most even heat in the cavity; the default recipes assume
TopGratins, browning cheese, finishing under the broiler, thin quick-cooking itemsHotter ambient air and proximity to the broiler element

The failure mode each position prevents is easier to remember than the rule. Pizza on the top rack gives you a soggy base with a browned top. A cake on the bottom rack gives you a dark, dense bottom before the middle sets. A gratin in the middle never develops its crust.

Using Two Racks at Once

Two dishes on two racks is where rack position starts mattering more than it usually does, because now the dishes are also affecting each other.

  • Offset them. Don't stack one pan directly above the other — the upper pan blocks heat and airflow to the lower one. Push one to the left and one to the right.
  • Leave an inch. Air needs to move around the pans and between them and the oven walls.
  • Use racks 2 and 4 (counting from the bottom in a five-slot oven), rather than the extreme top and bottom, so neither dish sits in the harshest position.
  • Swap at halftime. Trade the racks' positions and rotate each pan 180° at the midpoint. This is the single most effective thing you can do about oven unevenness.
  • Give the higher-temperature dish the rack that matches its need — bottom if it needs a crisp base, top if it needs a browned surface.

If the two dishes also call for different temperatures, that's a separate problem with its own arithmetic — see baking two things at different temperatures, or run it through the oven sharing calculator.

Convection Changes the Question

A convection fan circulates the air, which flattens the temperature difference between shelves. That's precisely why convection is the better setting when you're using more than one rack — it's closer to the point of the feature than the faster cooking people usually associate with it.

It doesn't eliminate hot spots, though, so rotating pans partway through remains worth doing. And the usual conversion still applies: reduce the temperature by about 25°F when switching a conventional recipe to convection, and start checking early. The same physics underlies why an air fryer crisps better than an oven — it's a small convection oven with a disproportionately strong fan.

Every Oven Has a Hot Side

Uneven heating is normal rather than a defect. Element placement, fan position, and where the cavity vents all create spots that run warmer, and even good ovens vary noticeably between the front and back or left and right.

Two things follow. First, rotating and swapping isn't fussiness — it's the correction for a known flaw in the equipment. Second, it's worth knowing how uneven yours is, which an oven thermometer moved around the cavity will tell you in one session. Our guide on whether your oven runs hot or cold covers the calibration side, which is a related but separate problem: calibration is about the whole oven being off, while hot spots are about parts of it disagreeing with each other.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the top or bottom oven rack hotter?

It depends which kind of heat you mean, which is why sources disagree. Most home ovens have their heating element at the bottom, so the lower rack gets more direct radiant heat. But hot air rises, so the ambient air near the top of the cavity is generally hotter and the top rack sits closer to the broiler. Bottom crisps bases; top browns surfaces.

Which rack should I use if a recipe doesn't say?

The middle. It's the most evenly heated part of the cavity and the position most recipes are developed on, so it's the safe default whenever the instructions are silent.

How should I arrange two dishes on two racks?

Offset them rather than stacking one directly above the other, so hot air can circulate between and around both. Leave at least an inch between the pans and the oven walls, and swap the racks' positions at the halfway mark since almost every oven has a hotter side and a hotter shelf.

Does convection change which rack to use?

It makes rack choice matter less, because the fan evens out the temperature differences between shelves. That's why convection is the better setting for multi-rack cooking. Rotating pans partway through is still worth doing, since the fan reduces hot spots rather than eliminating them.

Why is one side of my oven hotter than the other?

Nearly all home ovens have uneven spots caused by element placement, fan position, and how the cavity vents. It's normal rather than a fault. The fix is mechanical rather than diagnostic: rotate pans 180° and swap rack positions at the halfway point of the cook.
