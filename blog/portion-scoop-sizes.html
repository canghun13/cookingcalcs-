Portion Scoop Sizes: What #8, #12 and #16 Mean

October 2026 · 7 min read

The number on a scoop is roughly how many level scoops fill a quart. A #8 is ½ cup, a #12 is ⅓ cup, a #16 is ¼ cup — bigger number, smaller scoop. Once you know that, a scoop stops being a serving spoon and becomes a measuring tool: divide the batch by the scoop and you know how many people it feeds.

Portion scoops — dishers, in kitchen-supply language — are the spring-handled scoops used for ice cream, cookie dough, mashed potatoes and every school-lunch side ever served. They come colour-coded and numbered, and the numbers confuse almost everyone the first time because they run backwards. That's because they were never sizes. They are counts.

The Full Size Chart

The level measures below are from the USDA Food Buying Guide's table of scoop sizes, which is what school and institutional kitchens plan with. The fluid ounce column converts those measures (a cup is 8 fl oz, a tablespoon ½ fl oz).

Why the Numbers Run Backwards

The number is how many level scoopfuls make one quart. Eight half-cups fill a quart, so a ½-cup scoop is a #8; sixteen quarter-cups fill a quart, so a ¼-cup scoop is a #16. The higher the number, the more scoops per quart and the smaller each one. The relationship isn't exact all the way down the chart — a #10 is ⅜ cup, not quite a tenth of a quart — but it's close enough to reason with, and it's why the number is useful: it already tells you servings per quart.

Turning a Scoop Into a Headcount

Because a scoop is a fixed volume, servings per batch is a single division: batch volume ÷ scoop volume. Work in fluid ounces — a quart is 32, a gallon 128.

Run it the other way to choose a scoop. Want a 3-ounce serving of coleslaw? That's a #10. Need a pan of mashed potatoes to stretch to 60 people from 7½ quarts (240 fl oz)? 240 ÷ 60 = 4 fl oz each, so serve with a #8 and you'll come out even. Treat these as level-scoop counts and keep a margin — the last inch of any pan never scoops cleanly. Once you know the volume, the catering pan calculator tells you how many pans it fills.

Volume Isn't Weight

Scoops measure volume. A level #8 of fluffy rice weighs noticeably less than a level #8 of dense mashed potatoes, even though both are ½ cup. That matters when a recipe or a meal plan specifies portions in ounces by weight: the scoop gets you consistent servings, but to know the weight you have to put a few on a scale. For most home cooking and party planning, consistent volume is the thing you actually need — it's what makes a pan come out even.

Level versus heaped matters more than people expect. A heaped scoop can carry a lot more than its rated volume, which is exactly how a pan planned for 25 serves 18. Drag the scoop against the side of the pot to level it, or use a scoop with a sweeping spring arm and let it do the work.

Scoops for Baking

For bakers the main payoff is evenness: same-size cookies and muffins bake in the same time. A #16 (¼ cup) fills a standard muffin cup about half to two-thirds full; a #12 (⅓ cup) gives taller domes if the batter allows. For drop cookies, a #40 is the everyday size and a #30 makes bakery-style cookies. Changing the scoop changes how many you get from the same dough — 4 cups of dough is 38 cookies with a #40 but 32 with a #30 — and bigger cookies need a minute or two longer. If you're scaling a recipe to hit a set number of cookies, the scoop count is the number to scale to; recipe conversion factors covers the arithmetic.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a #8 scoop mean?

A #8 scoop holds ½ cup (4 fluid ounces) when level. The number is roughly how many level scoops fill a quart — eight half-cups make a quart.

How many tablespoons are in a #40 scoop?

1⅔ tablespoons, according to the USDA Food Buying Guide. A #30 is 2 tablespoons and a #24 is 2⅔.

What size scoop should I use for muffins?

A #16 (¼ cup) fills a standard muffin cup about half to two-thirds full. A #12 (⅓ cup) gives taller, domed tops.

How many servings does a gallon give with a #8 scoop?

32 — a gallon is 128 fluid ounces and a #8 holds 4. Plan for a few fewer, since the last of a pan rarely scoops cleanly.
