Recipe Conversion Factor (RCF) Word Problems With Answers

October 2026 · 8 min read

RCF = new yield ÷ old yield. Multiply every ingredient by it. The only trap worth worrying about: when the portion size changes, compare total yields (portions × portion size), never portion counts. Twenty 4-oz portions to fifty 3-oz portions is 150 ÷ 80 = 1.875, not 50 ÷ 20 = 2.5.

The recipe conversion factor is the culinary-school name for scaling a recipe, and it is usually taught with three kinds of problem: change the number of portions, change the number and size of portions, then turn the awkward decimals that come out into amounts someone can actually measure. The worked problems below go through each, then add the step most courses save for later — what an RCF should never be applied to.

The Formula

RCF = new yield ÷ old yield
New quantity = old quantity × RCF

"Yield" here means the total amount the recipe makes, in whatever unit the problem gives — portions, ounces, quarts or pounds. Both yields must be in the same unit before dividing.

Level 1: Changing the Number of Portions

A soup recipe yields 2 gallons. You need 5 gallons. What is the RCF, and how many pounds of onions do you need if the original uses 3 pounds?

RCF = 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5. Onions: 3 × 2.5 = 7.5 lb.

A chili recipe makes 40 portions and uses 2.5 pounds of ground beef. You need 15 portions. How much beef?

RCF = 15 ÷ 40 = 0.375. Beef: 2.5 × 0.375 = 0.9375 lb. Multiply by 16 to get ounces: 15 oz.

A blueberry muffin batter recipe yields 48 ounces. You need 80 ounces of batter. The recipe uses 2 cups of blueberries. What is the RCF, and how many blueberries?

RCF = 80 ÷ 48 = 1.667 (1⅔). Blueberries: 2 × 1⅔ = 3⅓ cups.

Level 2: When the Portion Size Changes Too

This is the version that appears on almost every culinary math test, because comparing portion counts gives a plausible-looking wrong answer.

A recipe makes 20 portions of 4 ounces. You need 50 portions of 3 ounces. The recipe uses 2 quarts of stock. How much stock do you need?

Old yield: 20 × 4 = 80 oz. New yield: 50 × 3 = 150 oz. RCF = 150 ÷ 80 = 1.875.

Stock: 2 × 1.875 = 3.75 qt, which is 3 quarts and 3 cups (0.75 qt × 4 cups per quart).

Comparing portions alone would give 50 ÷ 20 = 2.5 — a third too much, because the new portions are smaller.

A rice pilaf recipe makes 12 portions of 6 ounces using 1¼ pounds of rice. You need 30 portions of 5 ounces. How much rice?

Old yield: 12 × 6 = 72 oz. New yield: 30 × 5 = 150 oz. RCF = 150 ÷ 72 = 2.083.

Rice: 1.25 × 2.083 = 2.604 lb. The 0.604 lb × 16 = 9.7 oz, so about 2 lb 10 oz.

Level 3: Turning the Answer Into Something Measurable

An RCF almost never produces tidy numbers. The last step of every problem is converting the result into the units a kitchen actually uses.

Eggs are the one ingredient where weighing beats rounding: three large eggs are about 150g shelled, × 0.4 = 60g.

Level 4: An RCF Plus Trim Loss

A recipe for 8 portions uses 2 pounds of peeled, cubed butternut squash. You need 20 portions of the same size. Squash yields 66% after peeling and seeding. How much whole squash should you buy?

RCF = 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5. Prepped squash needed: 2 × 2.5 = 5 lb.

Whole squash to buy: 5 ÷ 0.66 = 7.58 lb, so about 7½–8 lb. The RCF tells you how much the recipe needs; the yield percentage tells you how much to buy to end up with it. The yield percentage calculator covers that second step for 51 ingredients.

What an RCF Should Never Be Applied To

ItemWhy it doesn't scale with the RCF
Oven temperatureA bigger batch needs the same heat, not more of it.
Cooking timeTime follows depth and thickness. Two pans of the original size cook in the original time; one pan twice as deep does not.
Pans and potsEquipment is fixed. Check the new volume fits before you start — the pot size calculator does this.
Salt, spices, leaveningScale them, but start lower at large RCFs and adjust. Strong flavours and chemical leaveners don't behave linearly in big batches.
EvaporationA wider pot loses liquid faster. Scaled sauces and soups may need less reduction time or a little more liquid.

Common Mistakes

Dividing the wrong way. Old ÷ new instead of new ÷ old. A quick check: making more food should give an RCF above 1; making less should give one below 1.

Comparing portion counts when the portion size changed. The level 2 problems show how large that error gets.

Mixing units. A yield of 2 gallons and one of 20 cups can't be divided until both are in the same unit — 2 gallons is 32 cups.

Stopping at the decimal. "2.625 cups" isn't an answer anyone can measure. Finish the conversion.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the formula for the recipe conversion factor?

RCF = new yield ÷ old yield. Multiply every ingredient by the RCF. If portion size changes as well as the number of portions, multiply portions by portion size for each version first, then divide.

A recipe makes 20 portions of 4 ounces. How do you convert it to 50 portions of 3 ounces?

Convert both to total yield: 20 × 4 = 80 oz and 50 × 3 = 150 oz. The RCF is 150 ÷ 80 = 1.875.

Is an RCF below 1 correct?

Yes — it means the recipe is being made smaller. If you get a number above 1 when you meant to reduce the recipe, the yields were divided the wrong way round.

What does the recipe conversion factor not change?

Cooking temperature, and usually not cooking time in proportion. Pans, pots and ovens don't scale either, and salt, spices and leavening are best scaled cautiously and adjusted by taste.
