Slow Cooker vs Pressure Cooker: Which to Use for What

August 2026 · 7 min read

These two appliances get compared constantly, almost always as a purchase decision. That's the less useful question. Most people who own one eventually own both, and the real problem is deciding which to reach for on a given evening — and knowing when a recipe written for one won't survive the move to the other.

They're closer than they look. Both are moist-heat methods, both excel at exactly the same category of food (tough, collagen-rich cuts that need connective tissue broken down), and both produce a similar result at the end. What differs is the route, and a few consequences of that route that matter more than the time saved.

The Core Difference in One Table

Slow CookerPressure Cooker
Temperature190-200°F (Low), 290-300°F (High)~250°F at high pressure
Typical braise8 hours on Low~24 minutes plus pressure time
EvaporationSome — sauces reduce over hoursEssentially none — sealed
Liquid neededLess; meat juices often sufficeMinimum ~1 cup thin liquid to make steam
Can you check on it?Yes, any timeNo, not while under pressure
Attention requiredNone once startedPresent at start and release
Overshoot riskForgiving — an extra hour rarely ruins itUnforgiving — a few minutes over is visible

The temperature row explains everything else. A pressure cooker exceeds the boiling point of water, which an open pot physically cannot do. That's the whole trick, and every other difference follows from the sealed environment that makes it possible.

Which One for Which Dish

DishBetter inWhy
Pulled pork, brisket, chuck roastEitherBoth break down collagen well — choose by time available
Dried beansPressure cookerDramatically faster, and reliable at altitude where boiling water is too cool
Stock and bone brothPressure cookerExtracts gelatin in about an hour instead of overnight
Chilli, stew with a thick sauceSlow cookerSauce reduces and concentrates; a sealed pot can't do this
Dairy-based saucesSlow cookerDairy can separate under pressure
Anything you'll be out of the house forSlow cookerSafe to leave running unattended for hours
Weeknight dinner decided at 6pmPressure cookerOnly realistic option at that hour
Delicate vegetables, seafoodNeither, ideallyBoth overcook them — add at the end

The Time Difference Is Smaller Than the Numbers Suggest

"Twenty-five minutes versus eight hours" is the headline, but pressure cooking minutes aren't clock minutes. The timer only starts once the pot reaches pressure, which takes 5 to 20 minutes depending on how full and how cold the contents are. Releasing adds more — a quick release vents in a couple of minutes, a natural release takes 10 to 20 as the pot cools on its own. Budget roughly 20 to 40 minutes beyond whatever the recipe's cooking time says.

So a "25 minute" pressure recipe realistically occupies about an hour. Still transformative compared with eight hours, but it changes how you plan side dishes, and it's the single most common surprise for people coming from a slow cooker. Our pressure cooker converter shows the conversion in both directions and flags this explicitly.

What Doesn't Convert Cleanly

Liquid usually goes up, not down. This catches people who've learned to cut liquid for the slow cooker. Both appliances trap moisture, but a pressure cooker needs a minimum volume of thin liquid to generate steam at all — commonly about 1 cup for a 6-quart pot. A slow cooker recipe relying on a roast's own juices may not clear that minimum.

Nothing reduces in a sealed pot. A slow cooker loses some moisture over eight hours and the sauce thickens as a result. A pressure cooker loses essentially none. If the original recipe expected concentration, simmer it down uncovered afterwards.

Thickeners go in at the end. Cornstarch slurries, roux, tomato paste, and heavy dairy can scorch on the bottom, trigger a burn warning, or separate. Pressure cook with thin liquid and stir thickeners in afterwards.

Texture isn't identical. Long slow cooking gives meat time to relax into a softer, more uniformly tender state; pressure cooking gets it tender fast but can leave a slightly more shredded, stringy texture on very lean cuts. For pulled pork nobody notices. For a roast you intend to slice, the slow cooker often holds together better.

The Energy Question

A pressure cooker draws far more power but for a fraction of the time; a slow cooker sips power but runs for hours. Consumer testing has generally found the pressure cooker comes out cheaper overall for most dishes, because the short runtime more than offsets the higher draw. It's a real difference but a modest one in absolute terms — not usually the deciding factor for a single meal, though it adds up if you cook this way most nights.

The Honest Summary

Pick by the clock and the sauce, not by which appliance is better. If you're leaving the house, or the dish depends on a sauce reducing, use the slow cooker. If you decided on dinner an hour ago, or you're cooking beans or stock, use the pressure cooker. For a standard braise with time either way, they land in nearly the same place — and the browning step you do beforehand will affect the result more than the appliance you finish it in.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a pressure cooker just a faster slow cooker?

For tough cuts, largely yes — both use moist heat to break down connective tissue and reach a similar end result. But they are not interchangeable for everything. A pressure cooker cooks in a sealed pot where nothing evaporates and nothing reduces, so dishes that depend on a sauce concentrating over hours don't translate directly.

Which one gives more flavour?

Neither is inherently better, but they build flavour differently. A slow cooker gives aromatics hours to meld and allows some liquid to reduce and concentrate. A pressure cooker extracts flavour aggressively in a short window but cannot reduce a sauce. Browning meat before either method matters more than the choice between them.

Can I use slow cooker recipes in a pressure cooker?

Most braises, stews, soups, beans, and tough cuts convert well. Convert the slow cooker time to its High equivalent, then multiply those hours by about 6 to get pressure cooking minutes — an 8-hour Low recipe becomes roughly 24 minutes. Check the liquid, since a pressure cooker needs a minimum volume of thin liquid to make steam.

Which uses less electricity?

A pressure cooker draws far more power but runs for a fraction of the time, while a slow cooker draws very little but runs for hours. Consumer testing has generally found the pressure cooker cheaper overall for most dishes because the short runtime outweighs the higher draw.

Do they reach the same temperature?

No. A slow cooker runs about 190-200°F on Low and 290-300°F on High. A pressure cooker reaches roughly 250°F at high pressure — above the boiling point of water, which is impossible in an open pot. That higher temperature is the entire reason it finishes so much faster.
