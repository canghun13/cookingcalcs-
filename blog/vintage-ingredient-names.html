Vintage & British Ingredient Names Decoded

Updated August 2026 · 6 min read

Old family recipes and British cookbooks both use ingredient names that don't match what's on American grocery store shelves. None of these are exotic substitutions — they're the same ingredients under a different name, or a very close modern equivalent.

Sugar & Sweeteners

Old/British TermWhat to BuyNotes
Caster sugarSuperfine sugarOr pulse granulated sugar in a food processor ~30 sec
Icing sugarPowdered/confectioners' sugarIdentical product, different name
Golden syrupNo exact US equivalentLight corn syrup + a little molasses is the closest substitute
TreacleMolasses"Black treacle" = dark molasses; "golden treacle" = golden syrup

Flour & Leavening

Old/British TermWhat to BuyNotes
Self-raising flourAll-purpose flour + baking powder + salt1 cup flour + 1½ tsp baking powder + ¼ tsp salt per cup called for
Plain flourAll-purpose flourDirect equivalent
SaleratusBaking soda19th-century American term, converts 1:1
Corn flour (UK)Cornstarch (US)Same product — "corn flour" in the US means cornmeal instead, so this one causes real confusion

Dairy & Fat

Old/British TermWhat to BuyNotes
Double creamHeavy creamDouble cream is slightly higher fat (~48% vs ~36%), whips a bit stiffer
Single creamLight cream / half-and-halfClose but not identical fat content
Shortening (older US recipes)Solid vegetable fat (e.g. Crisco)Same meaning today, just less commonly used now than in mid-century recipes
SuetBeef or vegetable suetStill sold, though less common in US supermarkets — look in the baking or specialty section

Produce & Vegetables

British TermWhat to BuyNotes
CourgetteZucchiniSame vegetable, French-derived British name
AubergineEggplantSame vegetable, different name on each side of the Atlantic
SwedeRutabagaBoth refer to the same root vegetable; "swede" is short for "Swedish turnip"
Spring onionScallion / green onionIdentical product, regional naming only
BeetrootBeetSame vegetable; "beetroot" specifically refers to the root, which is the only part typically eaten in the US anyway

Unlike the sugar, flour, and dairy terms above, these produce names rarely cause a recipe to actually fail if misread — swapping in "eggplant" for a recipe that says "aubergine" changes nothing about quantities or technique, since it's a straightforward name lookup rather than a different product or ratio.

The One That Causes the Most Confusion

"Corn flour" is the term most likely to cause a real mistake, not just a naming mix-up: in the UK, corn flour means cornstarch, but in the US, "corn flour" is a completely different product — finely ground cornmeal, used in cornbread and tortillas. If a British recipe calls for corn flour to thicken a sauce, reach for cornstarch, not the corn flour on a US grocery shelf, or the sauce won't thicken the way the recipe expects.

Why the Terms Diverged

Most of this naming split traces back to British and American English developing separately after colonization, combined with different manufacturing and marketing histories on each side of the Atlantic — sugar refiners, flour millers, and syrup producers in each country settled on their own product names decades apart, with no real coordination. A few terms (saleratus, "size of an egg") are simply older English that fell out of use in the US once standardized measuring tools and consistent product names took over, but stuck around longer in handwritten family recipes than in published cookbooks. Produce names diverged for a slightly different reason — courgette and aubergine both come from French, reflecting Britain's closer culinary ties to continental Europe, while American English kept the older English "eggplant" (named for pale, egg-shaped early varieties) and picked up "zucchini" from Italian immigration instead.

None of this means the ingredients themselves are meaningfully different — a British recipe using icing sugar isn't asking for something exotic, just powdered sugar by another name. The confusion is purely linguistic, not culinary, which is why a straightforward name-to-name lookup like the tables above resolves almost every case without needing to adjust the recipe itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is caster sugar in American terms?

Caster sugar is superfine sugar — granulated sugar ground finer than standard table sugar but not as fine as powdered sugar. If you can't find it, pulse regular granulated sugar in a food processor for about 30 seconds, or use standard granulated sugar; the difference is mainly how quickly it dissolves.

What is self-raising flour, and how do I make my own?

Self-raising (or self-rising) flour is regular flour pre-mixed with baking powder and salt. To substitute, use 1 cup all-purpose flour plus 1½ teaspoons baking powder and ¼ teaspoon salt for every cup of self-raising flour a recipe calls for.

What is saleratus?

Saleratus is an old term for baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) — the name was common in 19th-century American recipes before "baking soda" became the standard term. It's the same substance and converts 1:1 with modern baking soda.

What is a swede, and is it the same as a rutabaga?

Yes — "swede" is the British term for the same root vegetable Americans call rutabaga (short for "Swedish turnip," which is also where rutabaga's name comes from). If a British recipe calls for swede, buy rutabaga; the two words describe the exact same produce, not a substitution.
