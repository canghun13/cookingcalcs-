The Complete Appliance Cooking Guide

August 2026 · 7 min read

Four appliances cook most of what comes out of a home kitchen: the oven, the slow cooker, the pressure cooker, and the air fryer. They're usually discussed one at a time, as purchases. This guide treats them as one system — what each does to food, how a time in one translates to a time in another, and how to decide which to reach for on a given evening.

The One Distinction That Organises Everything

Every one of these appliances sits on one side of a single line: whether it cooks food with water or with dry air.

Moist heat — slow cooker and pressure cooker. Food cooks surrounded by liquid or steam. This dissolves collagen in tough cuts and makes them tender, but it can't brown anything, because browning requires a dry surface above the boiling point of water.

Dry heat — oven and air fryer. Hot air surrounds the food, moisture leaves the surface, and the surface browns and crisps. Excellent for texture, useless for breaking down a chuck roast in any reasonable time.

Almost every cooking disappointment involving these appliances traces back to asking one side of that line to do the other's job — expecting crisp skin from a slow cooker, or tender brisket from an air fryer. Within each pair, the appliances are largely interchangeable and times convert predictably. Across the line, they aren't substitutes at all.

What Each One Actually Does

ApplianceHeat TypeTemperatureBest At
Slow cookerMoist190-200°F Low / 290-300°F HighUnattended braises, sauces that need to reduce
Pressure cookerMoist~250°F at high pressureBeans, stock, tough cuts on a weeknight
OvenDryWhatever you setLarge cuts, batches, anything that rises
Air fryerDry350-400°F typicalSmall crisp items, reheating, single portions

The pressure cooker's ~250°F is the outlier worth noticing: it's above the boiling point of water, which no open pot can reach. That single fact is why it compresses an eight-hour braise into under half an hour.

How Times Convert

Within each heat type, conversion is reliable enough to do arithmetic on.

Slow cooker ↔ pressure cooker. Convert the slow cooker time to its High equivalent (1 hour High ≈ 2 hours Low), then multiply those hours by about 6 for pressure cooking minutes. An 8-hour Low braise is roughly 24 minutes at pressure. The pressure cooker converter runs this in both directions, and the slow cooker converter handles oven-to-slow-cooker times.

Oven ↔ air fryer. Reduce the oven temperature by about 25°F and cut the time by roughly 20%; reverse it going the other way. This is a fallback, not a rule — different foods want genuinely different air fryer temperatures, so the measured per-food settings in the air fryer cooking times chart are more accurate than any conversion.

Across the line, there is no conversion. Nothing turns a slow cooker time into an air fryer time, because the two aren't doing the same thing to the food. If you need to cross, you're changing the dish, not the timing.

Which One Tonight?

SituationUse
Leaving the house for the daySlow cooker — the only one safe to run unattended for hours
Decided on dinner an hour ago, tough cutPressure cooker
Dried beans or stockPressure cooker — dramatically faster, reliable at altitude
Sauce needs to thicken and concentrateSlow cooker — a sealed pot can't reduce
One or two portions, want it crispAir fryer
Reheating leftovers you want crisp againAir fryer
Feeding four or moreOven — air fryer batching erases its time advantage
Whole roast, sheet-pan dinner, anything that risesOven

The Mistakes Each One Invites

Slow cooker: too much liquid. Almost nothing evaporates, so a recipe adapted from the oven usually needs its liquid cut. Dairy and delicate vegetables also break down over eight hours — add them near the end.

Pressure cooker: forgetting the clock isn't the clock. Cooking time starts only once the pot reaches pressure, which takes 5-20 minutes, and release adds 10-20 more. Budget 20-40 minutes beyond the stated time. Liquid often needs to go up, not down, since the pot needs a minimum volume of thin liquid to make steam at all.

Air fryer: overcrowding. This causes more disappointment than any timing error. A packed basket blocks airflow and the food steams instead of crisping. Single layer, space between pieces, second batch if needed.

Oven: trusting the dial. Home ovens routinely run 15-25°F off their setting, and the difference between convection and conventional matters more than most recipes acknowledge. See the oven temperature converter for Gas Mark and old recipe terms.

What Doesn't Change, Whichever You Use

Safe internal temperatures are identical across every method — 165°F for poultry, 160°F for ground beef, 145°F for whole cuts of pork and beef with a rest. No appliance changes them, and a thermometer stays accurate in all of them. The meat temperature guide and the complete meat cooking guide cover the full set.

Altitude is the other constant worth knowing. It affects the moist-heat pair significantly, since water boils cooler as elevation rises, and it affects pressure cooking mildly. It doesn't affect oven roasting or air frying at all, because dry heat runs well above boiling either way. The high altitude cooking calculator works out the adjustments for your elevation.
