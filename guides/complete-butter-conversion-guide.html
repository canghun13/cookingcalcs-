The Complete Butter Conversion Guide

August 2026 · 7 min read

Butter is measured more inconsistently than any other common ingredient. A single recipe collection can call for it in sticks, cups, tablespoons, grams, ounces, or — if the recipe is old enough — a lump the size of an egg. This guide pulls every one of those into a single reference, and, more usefully, tells you which conversions you actually need to get right.

Start Here: One Line Solves Most of It

1 US stick = ½ cup = 8 tablespoons = 4 oz = 113g. Two sticks make a cup (227g); four sticks make a pound (454g). Nearly every butter question is arithmetic on that line, which is why it's worth memorising rather than looking up.

The wrapper does most of the remaining work. US sticks are printed with tablespoon markings, so cutting 3 tablespoons means cutting at the mark rather than measuring anything. If the wrapper is gone or the butter came as a block, the sections below cover it.

Salted and unsalted butter weigh and convert identically — the only difference is salt content. Brand doesn't change anything either, since the US stick size is a packaging convention rather than a manufacturer choice.

Butter by Where the Recipe Came From

The unit a recipe uses tells you where and roughly when it was written, and that's more useful than it sounds — it tells you what else to expect from the recipe.

How it's writtenOriginWhat to do
"2 sticks" / "1 cup butter"US / CanadaUse the table above. Volume-based; a scale is optional
"250g butter"UK, Europe, AustraliaWeigh it. 250g ≈ 2¼ sticks — the block is not two sticks
"200g butter"Continental EuropeWeigh it. ≈ 1¾ sticks
"Butter the size of an egg"Pre-1950 American¼ cup / 4 tbsp (~55-60g)
"A knob of butter"British, informal, still current~1 tbsp (~14g)
"A lump of butter"Older British~2 tbsp (~28g)
"Butter the size of a walnut"Vintage, for finishing1-2 tbsp (~15-25g)

The 250g block is the trap here. It looks like it should be two sticks — the shape is similar and the packaging is comparable — but 250g is about 2¼ sticks, and treating it as 2 sticks (227g) leaves you 10% short. In a cookie dough nobody notices; in shortcrust pastry it changes the texture. Full vintage terms are in Vintage Recipe Measurements Decoded.

When Butter Precision Actually Matters

Not every recipe cares. Knowing which do saves a lot of unnecessary fussing with scales — and tells you when the fussing is warranted.

Precision neededRecipesWhy
High — weigh itLaminated dough (croissants, puff pastry), shortcrust, shortbreadThe fat-to-flour ratio is the recipe; a 10% error changes the structure
Moderate — measure carefullyCookies, cakes, buttercreamAffects spread and crumb, but there's tolerance
Low — eyeball itSautéing, greasing pans, finishing sauces, butter on toastNothing structural depends on the amount

Two things matter more than the number in the high-precision row: European butter's higher fat content (82-84% vs 80% for US butter) and the temperature of the butter. Both change the outcome even when the weight is identical, and both are covered in How to Measure Butter.

Temperature Is a Separate Variable From Amount

Worth stating plainly because conversion charts imply butter is butter: 113g of cold, softened and melted butter are the same weight and completely different ingredients. Softened butter holds air when creamed, which is what makes cakes light. Melted butter can't hold air, which is why melted-butter cookie recipes come out chewy and dense on purpose. Cold butter stays in distinct pieces, which is what creates flaky layers in pastry.

So converting the amount correctly and then using the wrong temperature still gives you the wrong result. If a recipe specifies a state, that instruction is doing as much work as the quantity is.

SymptomLikely butter causeFix
Cookies spread into one sheetButter too warm, or too muchChill the dough; weigh rather than pack a cup
