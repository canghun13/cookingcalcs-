The Complete Culinary Math Guide

September 2026 · 11 min read

Culinary math is not a pile of formulas. It is four questions, and they only work in one order: how much do I need, how much do I buy, what does it cost, and what is a portion worth? Almost every kitchen arithmetic mistake is one of those questions answered before the one above it.

The Four Questions, in Order

OrderQuestionWhat decides it
1How much food do I need?Portions × portion size, and the recipe conversion factor
2How much do I buy?Yield percentage, then cooking shrinkage
3What does it cost?Edible portion cost, then portion cost
4What is a portion worth?Food cost percentage and menu price

Most people start at question 1, jump to question 3, and are surprised when the shopping list and the costing both come out short. The reason is that question 2 is where the losses live, and those losses feed forward into everything below them.

Question 1: How Much Food Do I Need?

The unit of an answer here is total yield, not portion count. Convert both versions of the recipe into the same measure — portions multiplied by portion size — and divide the one you want by the one you have. That ratio is the recipe conversion factor, and every ingredient gets multiplied by it.

Twenty portions at 4 oz is 80 oz. Thirty-five portions at 6 oz is 210 oz. The factor is 2.625, not the 1.75 you get from comparing 35 with 20.

What the factor does not cover is everything that refuses to scale with it: leavening and salt flatten out at high multiples, spices intensify, pans do not get deeper just because the batter doubled, and cooking time follows thickness rather than volume. The complete recipe scaling guide goes through what scales linearly and what does not, the recipe multiplier handles the ingredient list, and RCF word problems has worked examples of every variation.

Question 2: How Much Do I Buy?

This is the step that gets skipped, and the one that makes the other three wrong when it is. Between the shop and the plate there are two separate losses, and they happen in a fixed order.

Trim loss comes first. Peel, seeds, stems, cores, bone, the fat you cut away. What survives is the edible portion, and the share that survives is the yield percentage. Whole cauliflower is around 55%; onions are around 89%. To go from a recipe quantity to a shopping quantity you divide by that percentage — never add it. The yield percentage calculator covers 51 ingredients in both directions, and recipe says 2 pounds trimmed, how much do I buy covers the second half of the problem: turning a decimal weight into whole onions and squashes.

Cooking loss comes second. Moisture and rendered fat leave during cooking, and the figure is completely independent of trim. A pound of untrimmed spinach loses its stems down to roughly 12 ounces, then loses most of what is left in the pan. The raw to cooked weight converter handles that stage for meat, grains and vegetables.

Apply them in that order — yield first, shrinkage second — and a recipe written in cooked weights stops being a guess. Apply only one of them and you are wrong by the size of the other.

Question 3: What Does It Cost?

Cost inherits the losses from question 2. The price on the shelf is the as-purchased cost; the cost that belongs in a recipe is the as-purchased cost divided by the yield percentage. Broccoli at $1.80 a pound bought as whole heads is really $2.95 a pound of florets.

Once every ingredient is costed at its edible portion price, the recipe total divided by the portions it actually produced gives the portion cost. The word that matters there is actually: recipes are optimistic about their yield, and a batch that claims 18 portions but fills 16 plates has a portion cost 12.5% higher than the sheet says.

The cost per serving calculator works through a full example; culinary math formulas lays out each formula with its worked numbers side by side.

Question 4: What Is a Portion Worth?

Only relevant if you are selling the food, and it is the shortest step. Food cost percentage is portion cost divided by menu price. Working backwards from a target instead, menu price is portion cost divided by the target percentage: $2.70 at a 30% target gives $9.00.

That figure is a floor, not a price. It is the point below which the dish stops paying for itself, and everything else about pricing happens above it.

Over a week or a month, the same percentage is worked from the stockroom rather than the plate: beginning inventory plus purchases minus ending inventory is the food actually used, and that divided by food sales is the period's food cost. Food cost percentage word problems works through both versions, including staff meals and transfers to the bar.

Baker's Math: When Flour Is the Whole

Bread and pastry formulas use a fifth kind of arithmetic. Instead of scaling by portions, every ingredient is written as a percentage of the flour — flour is 100%, water might be 68%, salt 2% — so one formula fits any batch size. To make a set amount of dough, divide the dough weight by the sum of the percentages to get the flour, then multiply out. The baker's percentage calculator works in both directions, and baker's percentage word problems covers the textbook cases, from a single loaf to a poolish.

Units Underneath All of It

None of the four questions survive a bad unit conversion, and most culinary math worksheets are really unit conversion in costume. Weight to volume changes by ingredient — a cup of flour and a cup of sugar are not the same weight — so cups to grams is ingredient-specific by necessity. Ounces are worse, because a fluid ounce is a volume and an ounce is a weight, and recipes use both without saying which.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is culinary math?

The arithmetic of getting a recipe from a page to a plate at a known cost: scaling it to the yield you need, buying enough raw ingredient to survive trimming and cooking, and costing the result per portion.

What is the difference between yield percentage and shrinkage?

Yield percentage is loss before cooking — peel, bone, seeds, trim. Shrinkage is moisture and fat lost during cooking. They are separate losses and they stack, yield first and shrinkage second.

Do I need culinary math for home cooking?

The first two questions, yes — they decide what you put in the trolley and whether you run out mid-recipe. Portion cost is useful for budgeting. Food cost percentage and menu pricing only matter if the food is for sale.

Why does my recipe cost more than I calculated?

Almost always because it was costed from shelf prices. Divide each ingredient's price by its yield percentage before adding anything up; low-yield ingredients are usually the expensive ones, so the error compounds.

In what order should I do the calculations?

Scale first, then work out purchase quantities from yield and shrinkage, then cost the edible portions, then price. Each step uses the output of the one before it, so an answer found out of order is built on a number that has not been corrected yet.
