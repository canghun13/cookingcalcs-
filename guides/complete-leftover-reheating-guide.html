The Complete Leftover Reheating Guide

August 2026 · 7 min read

Reheating gets treated as a trivial step, which is why so much of it goes badly. It's actually a different problem from cooking, governed by different rules, and most reheating advice fails because it doesn't acknowledge that.

Reheating Is Not Re-Cooking

This is the idea everything else follows from. When you cook raw food, high heat has a job: drive moisture off the surface so a crust can form, while the inside slowly comes up to temperature. A leftover has already been through that. Its exterior is set and dry, and its interior is fully cooked.

So the goal has inverted. You're no longer building anything — you're moving heat into the centre while doing as little further damage to the outside as possible. Apply the original cooking temperature and you overshoot the surface long before the middle is warm. That single misunderstanding produces most dry chicken, leathery steak and scorched casserole edges.

The practical version: reheat roughly 30-50°F below whatever the food was cooked at. Fried chicken cooked at 400°F comes back at 375°F. A casserole baked at 375°F comes back at 350°F. Meat you want to stay medium-rare comes back at 250°F, barely warm enough to count as an oven.

The Two Temperatures

Reheating advice constantly mixes two numbers that have nothing to do with each other, and the confusion is worth naming directly.

What it isWho sets it
165°FInternal temperature the food must reach — the food safety targetUSDA. Fixed. You verify it with a thermometer
250-425°FThe oven or air fryer setting — a tool for controlling textureYou choose it, based on the food

You set the second and verify the first. Turning an oven to 165°F because that's the number in the guidance is a real and common mistake — the food sits for an hour drying out and may never get properly hot in the middle.

Choosing the Method

Every reheating decision reduces to one question: does the surface need drying out, or protecting?

GoalMethodFoodsMechanism
Restore crispAir fryer, then ovenPizza, fries, fried chicken, breaded items, roast potatoes, pastryMoving dry air carries surface moisture away
Protect moistureStovetop or covered ovenRice, pasta, casseroles, meat slices, fishAdded liquid and a cover replace what heat drives off
Even heat, no fussStovetopSoup, stew, chilli, saucesDirect heat plus stirring, no cold pockets
Speed onlyMicrowaveAnything, when texture doesn't matterFast, but pushes moisture toward the surface

The microwave isn't bad at reheating — it's bad at texture, and it's bad at evenness. For soup it's completely fine. For pizza it's working directly against the thing you want. Full per-food times across all four methods are in how long to reheat leftovers, and the reheating calculator shows the four side by side with the recommended one marked.

Why the Air Fryer Is So Good at This

An air fryer is a small convection oven with a strong fan, and reheating is arguably what it's best at — better than cooking from raw, where its capacity limits bite. Leftovers are usually single portions, which is exactly the scale it suits, and its concentrated airflow removes surface moisture faster than a full oven can.

The limits are the same ones covered in air fryer vs oven: it can't handle liquids, and once you need more than one basket load the oven wins. Crowding ruins reheating exactly as it ruins cooking — a packed basket traps the steam it's supposed to be removing.

The Safety Layer

Reheating has genuine food safety rules, and they're short.

  • 165°F internal for leftovers, steaming hot throughout. Soups, sauces and gravies to a rolling boil.
  • Microwave: cover, rotate or stir partway, then stand covered for two minutes. Microwaves heat unevenly enough that this rest genuinely matters.
  • Not in a slow cooker or warming drawer. These bring food up through the bacterial growth range too slowly. Use them to hold food hot after it's been properly reheated.
  • Reheat once. Take out only the portion you'll eat; each reheat-and-cool cycle sends the rest back through the danger zone.
  • 3-4 days in the fridge, then freeze. Frozen leftovers can be reheated without thawing.

The one that catches people: reheating does not undo the 2-hour rule. Food left at room temperature too long isn't rescued by getting hot later, because some bacteria produce toxins that heat doesn't neutralize. That's a discard decision, not a reheating decision.

Diagnosing a Bad Reheat

ProblemCauseFix next time
Hot edges, cold centreSetting too high, or microwave unevennessLower and longer; stir and stand 2 min
Dry and toughReheated at cooking temperatureDrop 30-50°F, cover, add a splash of liquid
Soggy, lost its crispMicrowave, or a crowded basketAir fryer or oven, single layer
Sauce split or oilyDry heat on a cream sauceStovetop, low, stir in a little milk
Steak now well-doneReheated hot250°F oven to warm, then a 60-second sear
Rice hard and dryNo added moistureSplash of water, covered, low heat

Almost every row resolves the same way: lower heat, more time, and a cover if moisture matters. Going hotter to save time is the single most reliable way to make reheated food worse.
