The Complete Protein Comparison & Swap Guide

July 2026 · 6 min read

CookingCalcs has five head-to-head protein comparisons, each answering one specific "can I use X instead of Y" question. This page is the index and the shortcut: a master table showing which pairs swap easily and which don't, plus the one rule that decides which category any new pair falls into before you even look it up.

The One Rule That Decides Everything

Every protein swap question comes down to a single check: do the two proteins share the same safe internal temperature? If they do, swapping is mostly a matter of adjusting time for size or shape. If they don't, the two proteins were never really interchangeable — you need the new protein's own time and temperature from scratch, not an adjustment to the old recipe's numbers.

Why "Same Temperature" Doesn't Always Mean "Same Time"

Three of the five pairs above share an identical safe temperature, yet none of them are true 1:1 swaps for time. That's because safe temperature and cook time are decided by two different things: temperature is about the pathogen the protein carries, while time is about how far heat has to travel to reach the center. A shrimp and a salmon fillet both stop at 145°F, but a shrimp is a fraction of the thickness, so it gets there in minutes instead of over ten. A turkey breast and chicken breast both stop at 165°F, but conventional wisdom cooks them at different oven temperatures entirely — 400°F for the small piece, 325°F for the large one — because a hot oven would scorch the outside of a turkey breast long before the center caught up.

When the Temperature Itself Is Different

Bacon and sausage are the one pair here where the underlying safety number changes — 145°F for cured bacon versus 160°F for raw ground sausage. This isn't a size or convention difference; it reflects that sausage is raw ground meat (bacteria can be distributed throughout, not just on the surface) while bacon is already cured. When two proteins have genuinely different safe temperatures like this, there's no shortcut — treat the swap as a completely different dish and look up the new protein's own numbers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the single most important thing to check before swapping one protein for another?

Whether the two proteins share the same safe internal temperature. If they don't (like bacon at 145°F versus sausage at 160°F), you can't reuse the original recipe's time or temperature at all — you need the new protein's own numbers from scratch. If they do share a safe temperature, the swap is usually just a time adjustment for size.

Why do some proteins need a different oven temperature even when the safe internal temperature is identical?

Oven temperature is chosen based on size, not the target internal temperature. A larger piece of meat needs a lower oven temperature so the outside doesn't overcook before heat reaches the center; a smaller piece can handle a hotter oven since heat only has to travel a short distance. Turkey breast and chicken breast both finish at 165°F, for example, but turkey breast uses a slower 325°F while chicken breast uses a faster 400°F, purely because of the size difference.

Is it ever safe to just split the difference between two proteins' temperatures?

No. Safe internal temperatures are food-safety minimums tied to the specific pathogens each protein can carry, not a stylistic choice — averaging or splitting the difference between two different targets can leave one of them undercooked. Always cook to the higher, correct minimum for whichever protein you're actually using.
