The Complete Vintage Recipe Guide

Updated August 2026 · 6 min read

Old family recipes and vintage cookbooks use measurements, can sizes, oven descriptions, and ingredient names that don't match anything on a modern grocery shelf or measuring cup. This page ties together everything CookingCalcs has for decoding them, so you can go from a handwritten card to a finished dish without guessing.

Can Sizes

Recipes from the mid-20th century and earlier often call for a can by number — "one #2½ can of tomatoes" — rather than a weight or cup measurement. The Can Size Converter covers every standard size from a small #1 picnic can up to a #10 institutional can, plus common EU metric sizes.

Measurement Terms

A gill, a teacup, a wineglass, "butter the size of an egg" — these were all standard before measuring cups and spoons were common kitchen equipment. Vintage Recipe Measurements Decoded converts every common term into exact cups, tablespoons, and grams.

Ingredient Names

Caster sugar, self-raising flour, saleratus, corn flour — some of these are British terms, some are simply old American ones. Vintage & British Ingredient Names Decoded maps each one to what to actually buy.

A General Rule for Precision

Can sizes and oven-temperature ranges are standard enough to trust directly. Object-based measurements (a teacup, an egg-sized lump of butter) were always approximate, even in the era they were written — treat those as a reliable starting point and adjust by texture and taste, the same way the original cook likely did.

Worked Example: Decoding a Real Vintage Recipe Line by Line

Here's a typical instruction from a handwritten 1940s recipe card, decoded piece by piece: "Cream a coffee cup of sugar with butter the size of an egg. Add a #2½ can of pumpkin. Sift in a saltspoon of salt and a pinch of ginger. Bake in a moderate oven."

Once every term is converted, the instruction reads like any modern recipe: cream 1 cup sugar with ¼ cup butter, add 3½ cups pumpkin, sift in ¼ teaspoon salt and a small pinch of ginger, bake at 350°F. Nothing about the actual cooking changed — only the labels needed translating. This is the general pattern for any vintage recipe: work through it once, term by term, before you start mixing, rather than converting mid-recipe when your hands are already in the bowl.

How to Roughly Date a Recipe by Its Terminology

The vocabulary a recipe uses is a rough clue to when it was written, which can help you decide how literally to trust its measurements. Numbered can sizes (#2½, #303) point to the early-to-mid 20th century, when the US canning industry's numbering system was the standard reference on recipe cards. "Saleratus" instead of "baking soda" usually signals an American recipe from the 19th century, since the term had mostly faded from common use by the 1930s. Gas Mark numbers (rather than °F) point to a British recipe, since gas ovens with numbered dials were standard in UK kitchens well before most American ovens used them. A recipe that mixes several of these — a numbered can size alongside "moderate oven" and a teacup measurement — is a good sign it predates standardized measuring cups altogether, since Fannie Farmer's push for precise, leveled cup and spoon measurements didn't fully take hold in American home kitchens until the early-to-mid 1900s.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where do I start when translating an old family recipe?

Read through the whole recipe first and flag anything unfamiliar — can sizes (like #2½), measurement terms (like a gill or teacup), oven descriptions (like "moderate oven"), and ingredient names (like caster sugar or saleratus). Look each one up before you start cooking rather than guessing mid-recipe, since a wrong guess on any one of these can throw off the whole result.

Are vintage recipe conversions exact?

Mostly close, not laboratory-exact. Can sizes and oven-term ranges are standard enough to trust directly. Object-based measurements like a teacup or "butter the size of an egg" were always approximate, even when the recipe was written, so treat those as a reliable starting point and adjust by texture and taste as needed.

Why did old recipes avoid exact measurements in the first place?

Standardized measuring cups and spoons weren't common household equipment until the early-to-mid 1900s. Before that, cooks measured against consistent household objects — a teacup, an egg, a can they already had on the shelf — because those were more universally available than a calibrated cup, and recipes assumed a practiced cook would adjust by feel.
