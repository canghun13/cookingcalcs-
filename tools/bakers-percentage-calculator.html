How Baker's Percentage Works

Baker's percentage — also called baker's math or formula percentage — writes every ingredient as a share of the flour, not of the whole dough. The flour is always 100%. Everything else is its weight divided by the flour weight, times 100.

Ingredient % = ingredient weight ÷ total flour weight × 100
Ingredient weight = total flour weight × ingredient % ÷ 100
Total flour weight = dough weight ÷ (sum of all % ÷ 100)

That is why the percentages add up to well over 100: a lean loaf of 100% flour, 68% water, 2% salt and 1% yeast totals 171%. It is also why the system is useful. Once a formula is in percentages it no longer belongs to one batch size — the same four numbers make one loaf or forty, and two formulas can be compared at a glance. A 68% dough and a 80% dough are different breads before you have weighed anything.

The calculation only works with weights. Cups of flour vary by 20% or more depending on how the cup is filled, so convert volume recipes first — the cups to grams converter uses 120 g per cup of all-purpose flour and 130 g per cup of bread flour.

Working Backward From the Dough You Want

Bakers usually know the finished size — four 250 g pizza balls, two 900 g loaves — and need the grams. Add the percentages, turn the total into a decimal, and divide:

Four 250 g pizza balls at 100% flour, 62% water, 2.8% salt, 0.2% yeast, plus 2% for dough left in the bowl.

Dough needed: 4 × 250 = 1,000 g, × 1.02 = 1,020 g. Sum of percentages: 165%.

Flour = 1,020 ÷ 1.65 = 618 g. Water = 618 × 0.62 = 383 g. Salt = 618 × 0.028 = 17.3 g. Yeast = 618 × 0.002 = 1.2 g.

The bench-loss allowance matters more than it looks. Dough sticks to the bowl, the bench and your hands; without 1–3% extra, the last ball is always light. For a single loaf it rarely matters, so leave it at zero.

Salt sits near 2% in almost every bread, and that is the most useful single check in baker's math: if a recipe works out to 4% salt, something was copied wrong. Instant yeast runs from a fraction of a percent in long, cold fermentations to 1–2% for same-day doughs. Hydration When the Liquid Isn't Water

Hydration is the water as a percentage of flour. With plain water it is just the water's baker's percentage. Milk, eggs and butter carry water too, but not all of it, so a milk dough at "60%" is drier than a water dough at 60%. The calculator counts them using USDA FoodData Central water contents:

Choose Other for sugar, salt, yeast, oil, seeds and anything else that carries little or no water. Sugar and fat also make a dough feel softer than its hydration suggests, which is why brioche handles like a wet dough at under 50% true hydration.

Mistakes That Give Wrong Percentages

Dividing by the total dough instead of the flour. 350 g water in an 870 g dough is 40% of the dough, but 70% in baker's percentage. Only the flour goes underneath.

Leaving one flour out. Whole wheat, rye, semolina and the flour inside a preferment all belong in the 100%. If a poolish of 200 g flour and 200 g water goes into the dough, both halves count toward the totals.

Rounding yeast and salt to whole grams in small batches. 0.2% of 618 g is 1.24 g; rounding it to 1 g cuts the yeast by nearly a fifth. A 0.1 g scale is worth having for pizza and long-fermented doughs.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate baker's percentage?

Divide each ingredient's weight by the total flour weight and multiply by 100. Flour is always 100%. A dough with 500 g flour and 350 g water is 350 ÷ 500 × 100 = 70% water.

How do you find the flour weight from a total dough weight?

Add up all the percentages, then divide the dough weight by that total as a decimal. For 1,700 g of dough at 100% flour, 68% water, 2% salt and 1% yeast, the total is 171%, so flour = 1,700 ÷ 1.71 = 994 g.

Why do baker's percentages add up to more than 100?

Because every ingredient is measured against the flour, not against the whole dough. Flour alone is 100%, so anything you add pushes the total above 100. A lean bread typically totals around 170%.

Does milk count toward dough hydration?

Partly. Whole milk is about 88% water, whole egg about 76% and butter about 16%. A dough with 60% milk behaves like roughly 53% water hydration, which is why milk doughs feel stiffer than a water dough at the same percentage.

What if a recipe uses two kinds of flour?

Add the flours together — the combined flour weight is 100%. A dough with 700 g bread flour and 300 g whole wheat flour is 70% bread flour and 30% whole wheat, and every other ingredient is a percentage of the 1,000 g total.
