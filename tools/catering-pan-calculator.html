The Pan Doesn't Decide the Servings — the Scoop Does

Ask how many people a half pan feeds and you'll hear "10 to 15", "about 20" and "25" in the same conversation. A long-running barbecue forum thread on exactly this question has one caterer saying the same pan feeds 60 to 70 people with baked beans but only about 40 with mac and cheese. Nobody is wrong. They are using different portions.

A deep full foil pan holds about 274 fluid ounces once you leave room at the top. At a 4-ounce scoop that is 68 servings — right where the 60 to 70 for beans comes from. People help themselves to more like 6 to 7 ounces of mac and cheese, and 274 divided by 6.5 is about 40. Same pan, same food volume, a 70% difference in headcount. Choose the portion first and the pan math follows.

Foil pan capacities are measured to the rim by the manufacturer; "in service" is 80% of that. Hotel pan figures use the low end of published ranges, which is what equipment suppliers recommend planning with for hot service. A 9×13×2 in dish is 234 cubic inches, about 4 quarts brimful.

Why Plan on 80%, Not the Rim

Capacities on pan sleeves and spec sheets are brimful: fill it to the very edge with water and that's the number. Nobody serves that way. A pan filled to the rim slops in the car, crusts over at the edges in a chafing dish and overflows the moment someone digs a spoon in. Foodservice suppliers advise holding food about an inch below the rim, which on a standard steam pan works out close to 80% of the rated volume. The calculator uses that figure for foil pans so the servings it gives are servings you can actually scoop.

Foods You Cut Instead of Scoop

Lasagna, baked ziti served in squares, sheet cake, cornbread and bars are counted by area, so depth doesn't matter. A half pan is about 12 × 10 in and a full pan 12 × 20 in.

A Worked Example

Forty guests, mac and cheese as one of several sides, served with a #8 scoop (4 ounces), with 10% extra for seconds: that's 44 servings. A deep foil half pan holds about 102 fluid ounces in service, which is 25 servings, so two half pans cover it with a few portions spare. One deep full pan (68 servings) would also do it — but two halves let you keep one hot in the oven while the other is on the table.

For lasagna as the main course, the same 40 guests with no seconds need 40 pieces. At 3 × 4 in, a half pan gives 9 and a full pan 20, so plan on two full pans.

Keeping a Buffet Safe

Hot food on a buffet needs to stay at 140°F or above, and anything that drifts into the 40–140°F range should go back to the kitchen or the bin within two hours — the same rule covered in how long food can sit out. This is the practical case for two half pans over one full pan: put one out, keep the other hot, and swap them rather than topping up a pan that has been sitting at room temperature.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many people does a half pan feed?

It depends on the portion. A deep foil half pan holds about 102 fluid ounces in service — about 25 servings at 4 ounces (a #8 scoop), 17 at 6 ounces, or 9 to 12 squares of lasagna or casserole.

How many servings are in a full pan?

A deep foil full pan holds roughly 274 fluid ounces in service: about 68 servings at 4 ounces or 45 at 6 ounces. Cut into squares, a full pan gives 20 to 24 pieces.

Is a full pan the same as two half pans?

Roughly — same footprint as two halves side by side, and a medium full foil pan holds about 232 fluid ounces against 107 for a medium half pan.

How full should a catering pan be?

About 80% of rim capacity, or roughly an inch below the rim. It travels, holds and serves better, and published capacities are usually brimful.
