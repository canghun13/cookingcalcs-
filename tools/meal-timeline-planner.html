The Subtraction

Every guide to timing a meal says the same three words — work backwards — and then leaves you to do the arithmetic while holding a knife. The arithmetic itself is simple:

Start prep at = serving time − rest − cook − prep

A chicken needing 15 minutes of prep, 80 minutes in the oven and 20 minutes of rest, for a 6:30pm dinner: it goes in the oven at 4:50, out at 6:10, and prep starts at 4:35. Run that for every dish, sort by start time, and the whole afternoon has a shape.

What makes it worth computing rather than eyeballing is that the dishes interact. Two of them want the oven at once. One of them frees the oven while it rests. A side that takes 20 minutes doesn't need thinking about until 6:10. Those relationships are invisible until the times are lined up next to each other.

Resting Time Is the Most Valuable Block in the Plan

A roast that rests for 30 minutes leaves the oven half an hour before you serve. That isn't a delay — it's an empty oven handed to you at precisely the moment every side dish needs heat, at whatever temperature you like.

Most meal-timing problems dissolve once you plan around that window instead of treating it as dead time. Roast potatoes go in when the meat comes out. Gratins get their browning pass. Anything cooked earlier gets brought back up. Cooks who skip the rest end up carving a roast that leaks its juices onto the board and serving lukewarm sides — losing on both counts.

Resting times sit in our meat temperature guide, and the reason carryover happens at all is covered in how to calculate cooking time.

When the Oven Can't Take It

The moment a plan usually breaks is two dishes needing the oven at overlapping times at temperatures that don't get along. The planner above flags this, and the fix depends on how far apart they are.

Temperature gapDo this
Under 50°FShare the oven at the midpoint and adjust both times — see the oven sharing calculator
Over 50°FMove one dish out of the overlap: cook it before the roast, or during the roast's rest
One dish can't compromisePastry, bread, soufflé and choux cook alone. Schedule them first, then reheat if needed

The escape hatch for nearly all of these is that most side dishes reheat well. Cooking something an hour early and bringing it back up for ten minutes costs very little — see how long to reheat leftovers — while trying to force everything into the same hour costs a lot.

Build In a Buffer

Two things reliably push a plan late, and neither is the recipe's fault. A fuller oven cooks more slowly, because the food absorbs heat and the dishes block airflow around each other — allow roughly 10% for two dishes and 15% for three. And prep takes longer than anyone estimates, especially when you're chopping for four dishes rather than one.

For a multi-dish meal, add 15 to 30 minutes of slack rather than scheduling to the minute. If it turns out you're early, that's a drink before dinner. If you're late with no buffer, everything after the delay is late too.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I work out what time to start cooking?

Start at your serving time and subtract backwards for each dish: first its resting time, then its cooking time, then its prep time. That gives the moment prep has to begin. Doing this for every dish and sorting the results tells you the order of the whole meal and which dish starts first.

Does resting time count as part of the schedule?

Yes, and it is the most useful block in the whole plan. A roast that needs 30 minutes of rest leaves the oven 30 minutes before serving, which hands you an empty oven at exactly the moment your sides need heat. Treating the rest as dead time is why sides so often arrive lukewarm.

How do I know if my plan fits in one oven?

Lay out each oven dish's in-and-out window and look for overlaps. Two dishes overlapping at similar temperatures is usually fine. Two overlapping dishes needing temperatures more than about 50°F apart is a genuine conflict — move one earlier, cook it before the roast, or reheat it at the end.

Why does everything take longer than my plan says?

Two reasons usually. A fuller oven cooks more slowly because the food absorbs heat and blocks airflow, and prep almost always takes longer than people estimate — especially chopping for several dishes at once. Build in a buffer of 15 to 30 minutes for a multi-dish meal rather than scheduling to the minute.

What should I cook first when everything needs the oven?

Anything that needs resting, since it frees the oven while it rests, and anything that reheats well. Cook the dishes that lose their texture fastest — roast vegetables, anything crisp — last, so they come out closest to serving.
