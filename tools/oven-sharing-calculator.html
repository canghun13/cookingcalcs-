How Far Apart Is Too Far?

The compromise only works while both dishes stay inside their tolerance. Past that, you're not sharing an oven — you're cooking two dishes badly.

Gap between temperaturesVerdictWhat to do
0-25°FShare freelySet the midpoint. Time changes are small enough to ignore for most dishes
26-50°FShare with adjustmentSet the midpoint, adjust each time, stagger the start, check early
51-75°FMarginalUse the lower temperature and extend the hotter dish. Accept less browning on it
Over 75°FDon't shareCook in sequence. Hold the first dish covered, or reheat it briefly at the end

The Time Formula

When you move a dish off its intended temperature, the time moves with it. The estimate in wide use is straightforward:

New time = original time × (original temp ÷ new temp)

A gratin that wanted 30 minutes at 400°F, cooked at 425°F instead: 30 × (400 ÷ 425) ≈ 28 minutes. A casserole that wanted 45 minutes at 350°F, now at 375°F: 45 × (350 ÷ 375) = 42 minutes.

It's an estimate, not physics — real cooking time depends on the dish's density, depth, and starting temperature as much as on the oven setting. Published rules of thumb vary from "10-15% per 25°F" to this ratio, which for a 25°F change gives closer to 6-7%. Use the number as the moment to start checking, and finish on doneness cues or a thermometer rather than the timer.

Which Rack for Which Dish

Advice on this contradicts itself constantly — some sources say the top rack is hottest, others say the bottom. Both are describing something real, and the reason is that ovens deliver two different kinds of heat.

RackWhat it gives youPut here
BottomDirect radiant heat from the element belowPizza, pie, bread — anything needing a crisp base
MiddleThe most even heat in the cavityCakes, casseroles, roasts, anything uncertain
TopHotter ambient air, closer to the broilerBrowning tops, gratins, finishing under a broil

So when two dishes share an oven, put the one that wanted the higher temperature on the rack that suits what it needs from the extra heat — the bottom if it needs a crisp base, the top if it needs a browned surface. Stagger them left and right rather than stacking one directly above the other, so air can move, and swap their positions at the halfway mark since nearly every home oven has a hot side.

What Not to Compromise

Some dishes treat temperature as a structural instruction rather than a speed setting. Puff pastry needs high heat to convert its water to steam fast enough to push the layers apart. Choux needs it to set before it collapses. Soufflé and bread rely on a specific initial rise. Drop the temperature on any of these and you don't get a slower version — you get a different, worse result.

These get the oven to themselves. Everything else in the meal can go in before or after, and most sides reheat well enough that sequencing costs you very little. Our reheating times cover bringing an early dish back up to temperature at the end.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you bake two things at different temperatures at the same time?

Usually yes, if the two temperatures are within about 50°F of each other. Set the oven to roughly the midpoint and adjust each dish's time — the one that wanted the higher temperature needs longer, the one that wanted lower needs less. Beyond a 50°F gap the compromise degrades both dishes, and cooking them in sequence gives a better result.

How do I adjust cooking time when I change the oven temperature?

A widely used estimate is to multiply the original time by the original temperature divided by the new temperature. A dish that needed 30 minutes at 400°F, cooked at 425°F instead, becomes 30 × (400 ÷ 425) ≈ 28 minutes. Treat the result as a checkpoint to start testing doneness, not a finish line.

Does cooking two dishes at once make everything take longer?

Yes, modestly. More food in the cavity absorbs more heat and blocks airflow, and the door is open longer while you load. Budget roughly 10% extra for two dishes and 15% for three, then check doneness rather than trusting the clock.

Which oven rack is hotter, top or bottom?

Both, in different ways, which is why advice on this conflicts. In most home ovens the heating element sits at the bottom, so the lower rack receives more direct radiant heat — good for crisping bases. But hot air rises, so the ambient air near the top of the cavity is generally hotter and the upper rack is closer to the broiler, which is better for browning tops.

Which dishes should never share an oven?

Anything where the temperature is doing structural work: puff pastry, pie crust, choux, soufflé, and bread that needs a specific initial oven spring. These rely on hitting an exact temperature at an exact moment, and a compromise setting changes the result rather than just the timing. Cook them alone and use the oven for other dishes before or after.
