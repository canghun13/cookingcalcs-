How to Use This Calculator

Add your ingredients with their amounts and units, set the original serving count and your target serving count, and the calculator scales every ingredient instantly. You can add as many ingredients as you need.

What Doesn't Scale Perfectly

Most ingredients scale linearly — double the recipe, double the flour. But a few require judgment:

  • Salt and spices: Start with 75% of the calculated amount and taste. Flavor compounds don't scale exactly, especially with strong spices like cayenne or cinnamon.
  • Baking powder and baking soda: Scale these proportionally, but for very large batches use slightly less than the calculated amount to avoid over-leavening.
  • Eggs: You can't use half an egg easily. For half-recipes requiring 1 egg, beat the egg lightly and use half. For odd amounts, adjust with a small egg or extra liquid.
  • Cooking time: Bigger batches don't always take proportionally longer. A doubled cake in a larger pan takes longer, but two pans of cookies bake in the same time as one.

Scaling Recipes for Large Batches

Scaling a recipe by 4x or more for a party or freezer meal prep needs more than simple multiplication. Cooking equipment becomes the real constraint — a stockpot or sheet pan has a maximum capacity, and cramming in more food than it can hold evenly changes cook times unpredictably. When scaling past 3x, it's often more reliable to cook in multiple batches at the original size than to try to scale the equipment along with the recipe. Searing meat is a good example: a crowded pan steams instead of browns, so a 4x batch of a seared protein recipe usually needs 2-4 separate pans or rounds, not one giant pan.

Seasoning is the other place large-batch scaling breaks down fastest. Salt in particular doesn't scale perfectly linearly by taste once you're working with very large volumes — season to about 80% of the calculated amount, then taste and adjust at the end. This matters more the larger the batch gets, since a small percentage error compounds into a large absolute amount of extra salt.

Common Mistakes When Scaling a Recipe

The most common mistake is scaling every ingredient evenly, including the ones that don't multiply well by feel — garlic, chili, and other strong aromatics are usually best under-scaled slightly and adjusted to taste, similar to salt. The second common mistake is not adjusting cook time or pan size at all, which can leave a doubled recipe undercooked in the center even though the surface looks done. The third is forgetting that scaling changes ratios in ways that matter specifically for baking — see the pan size section below for why bake time doesn't scale at the same rate as ingredient quantity.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I double a recipe?

Set the original servings to the recipe's yield and the target servings to double that number. The calculator multiplies every ingredient by 2. For baked goods, you'll also need a larger pan.

How do I make a recipe for 1 person?

Set the original servings to whatever the recipe makes, and the target to 1. The calculator divides everything accordingly. Keep in mind that egg amounts may need manual adjustment.

Can I use this for baking?

Yes, but scale with care. Baking is chemistry — salt, leavening, and eggs should be adjusted thoughtfully rather than mechanically. Use the calculator as a starting point, then apply the tips above.

Adjusting Pan Size When Scaling a Recipe

Scaling ingredients up or down often means the pan needs to change too, and pan area — not just recipe size — determines bake time. Doubling a recipe baked in the same size pan means a thicker layer that needs more time at the same temperature; using a proportionally larger pan keeps the layer thickness similar, so bake time stays closer to the original. As a rule of thumb, if you increase pan area by X%, expect bake time to increase by roughly half that percentage, and check doneness a few minutes early rather than trusting the original time exactly.

Do sauces and liquids scale the same way as solid ingredients?

Mostly yes, but reduction times don't scale linearly. A doubled batch of sauce has more volume to reduce, so it may take noticeably longer to thicken to the same consistency than simple math would suggest — start checking texture well before the "doubled" time estimate.

What if my scaled recipe results in awkward fractional amounts?

Round to the nearest practical measurement your tools support — for example, 1.33 cups rounds cleanly to 1⅓ cups, while 0.83 tablespoons is close enough to round to ¾ or 1 tablespoon depending on how sensitive the ingredient is. For strong flavors like salt, spices, or leavening, round down rather than up if you're unsure, since it's easier to add more after tasting than to fix an overpowering batch.
