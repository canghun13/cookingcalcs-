The Two Numbers People Confuse

Reheating advice mixes up two completely different temperatures, and it causes a lot of dry, disappointing food.

What it isTypical value
165°FInternal temperature the food must reach — a safety target you measure with a thermometerFixed, for leftovers
300-400°FThe setting you put the oven or air fryer on — a tool, chosen for textureVaries by food

You set the second one; you verify the first one. Turning the oven to 165°F because that's the number you read is a common and genuinely counterproductive mistake — the food sits for an hour and dries out without ever getting hot in the middle.

Reheat Cooler Than You Cooked

The single most useful principle: a reheat setting should be roughly 30-50°F below the temperature you originally cooked the food at. Raw food needs aggressive heat to drive surface moisture off and build a crust. A leftover already has that set, dry exterior — hit it with full cooking heat and the outside toughens or burns well before the centre is warm.

This is why fried chicken that was cooked at 400°F reheats best around 375°F, and why a casserole baked at 375°F comes back at 325-350°F. It's also why the microwave, which does the opposite of drying a surface, is the wrong tool for anything you want crisp.

Crisp or Moist: Pick the Method First

Every reheating decision comes down to one question — does this food need its surface dried out, or protected?

GoalFoodsMethodWhy
Restore crispPizza, fries, fried chicken, wings, breaded items, roast potatoes, pastryAir fryer, then ovenMoving dry air pulls surface moisture away, re-crisping the coating
Protect moistureRice, pasta, casseroles, roast meat slices, fishStovetop or covered ovenAdded liquid and a cover replace the water that reheating drives off
Just get it hotSoup, stew, chilli, curry, saucesStovetopEven heat, easy stirring, no hot and cold pockets
Speed over textureAnything, when you have two minutesMicrowaveFastest, but adds surface moisture rather than removing it

The microwave isn't bad at reheating — it's bad at texture. For soup it's fine. For a slice of pizza it's working directly against what you want.

Food Safety, Briefly

USDA guidance for leftovers is 165°F internal, measured with a thermometer, with the food steaming hot throughout. Sauces, soups and gravies should be brought to a rolling boil. In a microwave, cover the food, rotate or stir partway through, and let it stand covered for two minutes afterwards so the temperature evens out — microwaves heat unevenly enough that this rest genuinely matters.

Two things worth knowing beyond the number. Slow cookers and warming drawers are not recommended for reheating, because they bring food up through the bacterial growth range too slowly; use them to hold food hot once it's already been reheated properly. And reheating doesn't undo the 2-hour rule — food that sat out too long isn't rescued by getting hot later.

Frequently Asked Questions

What temperature should leftovers be reheated to?

USDA guidance is 165°F (74°C) internal temperature for leftovers, measured with a food thermometer, and the food should be steaming hot throughout. This is the temperature inside the food, not the setting on your oven — those are two different numbers that are easy to confuse.

Why should I reheat at a lower temperature than I cooked at?

Raw food needs high heat to drive moisture off the surface and build a crust. A leftover already has a set, dry exterior, so hitting it with the original cooking temperature burns or toughens the outside before the centre is warm. A reheat setting roughly 30-50°F below the original cooking temperature is the usual starting point.

Is it safe to reheat leftovers more than once?

Each reheat-and-cool cycle sends the food back through the temperature range where bacteria multiply, so the safest practice is to reheat only the portion you intend to eat and keep the rest cold. Repeated cycles also degrade texture badly, especially for rice, pasta and fish.

Do I need to thaw frozen leftovers before reheating?

Not necessarily. USDA guidance says frozen leftovers can be reheated without thawing in a saucepan, microwave, or oven, though it takes longer. If you do thaw first, use the refrigerator, cold water, or the microwave — never the counter.

Why does the microwave make food soggy and unevenly hot?

Microwaves heat by exciting water molecules, so moisture moves toward the surface instead of evaporating away — the opposite of what crisping needs. They also heat unevenly, which is why food safety guidance says to cover, rotate or stir partway, and let the food stand covered for two minutes so the temperature evens out.
