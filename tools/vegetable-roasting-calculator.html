How the Timeline Works

The arithmetic is simple, but almost nobody does it in their head while holding a chopping board. Take the longest-cooking vegetable on your pan — that sets the total. Every other vegetable goes in when the time remaining equals its own roasting time.

Potatoes at 35 minutes and asparagus at 12: the potatoes start alone, and at the 23-minute mark (35 − 12) the asparagus joins them. Both finish at 35. That's it. The calculator just runs it for however many vegetables you've picked and sorts them into a running order.

Where charts elsewhere tell you to "start the hard vegetables first and add the soft ones later," this gives you the actual clock times, which is the part that decides whether dinner works.

Cut Size Beats Vegetable Type

Roasting time tracks the thickness of the piece far more than the identity of the vegetable, and this is the lever most people don't use. A potato in ¾-inch cubes takes about 35 minutes; the same potato in ½-inch cubes is closer to 25. Halve a Brussels sprout and it roasts in the time a whole one needs to merely warm through.

So if you want two vegetables to finish together and don't fancy staggering, cut the dense one smaller and the soft one chunkier. Carrots in thin coins and zucchini in thick half-moons will land within a few minutes of each other. The times in the chart above assume the cut listed beside them — change the cut and you change the time.

The Crowding Rule

The most common failure isn't timing, it's a full pan. Vegetables release a lot of water as they heat, and that steam has to go somewhere. On a crowded pan it sits between the pieces and cooks them in their own moisture — you get pale, limp vegetables no matter how long you leave them in or how hot the oven is.

Single layer, visible gaps between pieces, and a second pan rather than a taller heap. If you're running two pans, swap their rack positions halfway through, since almost every home oven has a hotter and a cooler shelf. Wet vegetables — anything just washed, or frozen — should be patted dry first, because that surface water has to boil off before browning can start at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I roast vegetables with different cooking times together?

Work backwards from the slowest vegetable. Start the longest-cooking one alone, then add each remaining vegetable when the time left on the clock matches its own roasting time. If potatoes need 35 minutes and asparagus needs 12, the asparagus goes in at the 23-minute mark so both finish together.

What temperature should I roast vegetables at?

425°F is the most widely recommended default and the basis for the times here. 400°F gives softer results and needs roughly 15-20% longer; 450°F crisps harder and finishes roughly 15-20% sooner but burns delicate vegetables. Leafy vegetables like kale do better around 350°F.

Why do my roasted vegetables come out soggy instead of crispy?

Almost always overcrowding. Vegetables release steam as they cook, and a packed pan traps it so they steam rather than roast. Spread them in a single layer with visible gaps, use a second pan rather than piling one, and pat wet vegetables dry before oiling.

Does cut size change roasting time?

Substantially, and more than most charts acknowledge. Roasting time tracks the thickness of the piece, not the vegetable itself. Potatoes cut into ¾-inch cubes take around 35 minutes, but the same potato cut into ½-inch cubes is closer to 25. Cutting dense vegetables smaller is the simplest way to make them finish alongside softer ones.

Should I use convection for roasting vegetables?

Convection helps, because moving air carries away the steam that causes sogginess. The usual adjustment is to drop the temperature about 25°F and check earlier, so a 425°F conventional roast becomes roughly 400°F on convection with the time shortened a little.
